Reach the second level. That’s the phrase I’d underline, because it’s the part people skip.

The first level is the defensive line. Any competent blocking gets a back past the line of scrimmage sometimes. The second level is the linebackers, and the only way a four-yard run becomes a thirty-yard run is if somebody gets off a first-level block and climbs to a linebacker before the back arrives.

A hundred and fifty-five yards almost certainly means that happened repeatedly, and it’s the single hardest thing offensive linemen do in the run game.

The six-man line is on film now.

And that gives me tell number twenty-seven. For the first time in a month, it’s not about a video. It’s about a football team.

The formation that only means one thing.

Here’s the argument, and it starts with arithmetic that cannot be escaped.

A football team puts eleven men on the field. Always eleven. Which means personnel is zero-sum, exactly like a fifty-three man roster and exactly like the box count I’ve been describing for weeks.

So when Dallas puts a sixth offensive lineman on the field, that man is occupying a spot that would otherwise hold a receiver or a tight end. You cannot have six linemen and a full complement of pass-catchers. The arithmetic forbids it.

Which means heavy personnel announces itself.

A defensive coordinator watches an extra offensive lineman jog onto the field, counts bodies, recognizes the grouping, and knows before the snap that this is probably a run. And the counter is trivial. If the offense adds a blocker, the defense adds a defender to the box.

Remember what I said about Fangio. A light box is not a philosophy, it’s a bet on arithmetic. Well, the arithmetic runs both directions. A heavy box is also a choice, and it is exactly the choice you make against heavy personnel.

So Monday night worked partly because of the sixth lineman and partly because it hadn’t been seen. Arizona has a week and a film copy.

And here is where it becomes a tell, in precisely the sense I’ve been using the word all month.

A tell is a signal that gives away information the signaler didn’t intend to give.

I’ve spent a month cataloguing tells in content. A headline that supplies an emotion. A title that converts a proposal into a decision. A video whose opening sentence you recognize. Every one of them is a signal that reveals something about how the thing was made.

A personnel grouping that only ever means one thing is the same object, in football.

If Dallas only runs out of heavy personnel, then heavy personnel is a tell, and the defense reads it for free, and the advantage evaporates. The formation has stopped manufacturing an advantage and started broadcasting an intention.

And that produces the test, and it is the sharpest version of my Arizona condition.

The six-man line only works as long as Dallas can also throw from it.

Not often. Not well, necessarily. Just enough that a defensive coordinator cannot load the box on sight. A couple of throws a game out of heavy personnel is the entire cost of keeping the run game alive, because it converts a signal back into a question.

So yesterday I said the number to watch against Arizona is rushing attempts while the score is within a touchdown, and I stand by that. But today I can make it better, and this is the real one.

Watch whether Dallas passes out of heavy personnel.

Because that single thing separates a trick from an identity.

A trick is a formation that worked once against a defense that wasn’t ready. An identity is a formation a defense has to respect even when it knows what’s coming.

And remember what started this whole thread. Four separate sources last week asked Dallas for variance. Kurt Warner wanted formation and motion variability. One analyst wanted Pickens moved around. Another wanted counters and pulling blockers to give the defense something else to react to. And the offensive coordinator said the offense was still finding an identity.

A formation you only run from is not variance. It’s a tell. Which means the sixth lineman is only an answer to the four-source convergence if it carries more than one meaning.

And this video, independently, arrives at almost the same place. It says the best coaches don’t simply repeat whatever worked on Sunday, that they understand why it worked, anticipate how the next opponent will respond, and develop alternatives when the initial plan stops producing results.

Anticipate how the next opponent will respond. That’s the counter-adaptation problem and it’s the whole of tell twenty-seven, stated from the coaching side rather than the defensive side.

Now the third number, and it’s the best individual stat line I have been handed all season.

Joey Porter Junior was targeted nine times and allowed two catches for ten yards.

Let me do that arithmetic because it’s absurd.

Two completions on nine targets is a twenty-two percent completion rate when thrown at. Quarterbacks across the league complete somewhere around sixty to sixty-five percent against a typical cornerback.

Ten yards on nine targets is one point one yards per target.

Ten yards on two catches is five yards a catch, which means neither of the two completions did anything either.

And here is why that line is more valuable than almost any coverage statistic you’ll ever see, and it goes back to something I taught weeks ago.

I told you there are three reasons a cornerback isn’t targeted, and only one of them is that he’s good. The other two are that he’s playing a side quarterbacks avoid for structural reasons, and that his help makes the throw unappealing. And I said targets per coverage snap is the number that separates them.

Nine targets removes that entire problem.

He was tested. Repeatedly. Philadelphia threw at him nine times in one football game, which is a lot, and he gave up ten yards. You cannot run the he wasn’t challenged argument against that line, because the challenge is in the numerator.

Now the caveats, because I owe you those too.

It’s one game. Nine targets is a real sample for an afternoon and a tiny one for a season, and coverage numbers are noisy because a single blown play can swing them.

And here’s the structural caveat I think matters most. A cornerback’s coverage numbers are partly a measure of his pass rush.

If a quarterback has five seconds, nobody covers anybody. Eventually a receiver uncovers and the ball goes there. So good coverage statistics always have some pass rush in them, and bad ones always have some pass rush in them too.

Which means I’d want Monday’s sack total before I decided how much of that line belongs to Porter.

But notice which direction that caveat runs, given what we know. Dallas had eight sacks in six games going into this, which is near the worst in football. If the pass rush was its usual self, then two catches for ten yards on nine targets was produced largely by the cornerback, and the number gets more impressive rather than less.

Now, something this video does structurally that I want to point out, because it bears on the framework I built yesterday.

This video is openly an aggregation. It says according to the supplied transcript. It says the source describes. It says the original report questions. It says reportedly, repeatedly. And at one point it says, about the Porter coverage numbers, if those numbers accurately reflect the game being discussed.

Yesterday I built a five-tier source hierarchy, and tier five was an aggregator floating an idea, and I was fairly dismissive of it.

I need to add something. Honesty about your tier is itself a quality signal.

A tier-five source that says according to the supplied transcript is doing something genuinely useful, because it’s telling you exactly how far its own knowledge extends. It has flagged itself as derivative, which means you know to go upstream before you rely on it.

Compare that to a tier-five source that says confirmed.

Same tier. Same amount of actual knowledge. Opposite relationship to the truth. And the difference is visible in the hedging, which means the hedging is information rather than weakness.

So the hierarchy needs a second dimension. Not just how close a source is to the knowledge, but whether it tells you how close it is.
