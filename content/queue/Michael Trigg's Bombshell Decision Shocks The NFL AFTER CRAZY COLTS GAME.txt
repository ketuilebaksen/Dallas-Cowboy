Start with the question, because the video’s first observation is the sharpest one.

And notice the small word the reporter chose. Sometime. What are his chances of seeing the field sometime. Nobody asks sometime about a player they expect on Sunday. Sometime is the word you use when you’ve stopped asking about this week.

Now the thing that makes the whole answer legible, and it’s an arithmetic observation rather than a literary one.

For James Mitchell, the coach gave a number. Thirty-eight plays. And a role. A veteran who can play multiple spots.

For Michael Trigg, in the same answer, he gave no number at all.

That’s the comparison that does the work. Not the warmth, which was real, but the specificity. One man got a snap count and a job description. The other got adjectives.

And I’d generalise it, because this is a tool you can use on any quote from any industry.

Count the nouns and the numbers, not the adjectives.

Praise is cheap and precise praise is not. A coach who says a man played thirty-eight plays has told you something checkable. A coach who says a man is doing a really good job has told you he was asked a question.

Now the verb observation, which is the best piece of close reading anybody has sent me this month and which I want to pass on exactly.

Every verb in that answer about Trigg points to the present or the past. Michael is doing a good job. He missed some time. You feel him out there. You see the ball skills.

Not one verb points to the future. No will. No going to. No soon.

For Trigg, the entire answer lives in the present tense.

I don’t think that was planned word for word either, and the video says the same, which is the right amount of caution. But it is a very precise way of managing an expectation without saying a single negative sentence, and it gives you a tool worth keeping.

When you want to know whether somebody is in the plan, check the tense.

A plan is a future-tense object. If every sentence about a man is about what he is and what he has done, and none of it is about what he will do, then the person speaking has not placed him in a future.

And now the phrase that I think is the actual answer to the reporter’s question, hidden in a compliment about somebody else.

Now the piece that makes the fan argument geometrically correct rather than merely emotional, and this is mine.

The coach said on Friday that he expects Indianapolis to bring a lot of blitz packages, with Lou Anarumo calling that defense.

Last week I explained the geometry of beating a blitz. Pressure takes defenders out of the middle of the field, because the middle is where linebackers live and linebackers are the cheapest blitzers. So a blitz creates space behind it, not beside it. Slants, crossers and intermediate digs attack the ground the blitzer vacated. Bubble screens go sideways and attack ground nobody left.

Now ask what kind of player lives in the space behind a blitz.

A big target, releasing late, into the middle of the field, who can make a defender miss after the catch.

That is a tight end with receiving traits. Specifically, that is the exact description the head coach gave of Michael Trigg, aimed at the exact weakness Indianapolis is about to attack.

So the fans did not pick this argument out of thin air. The traits the coach named are the traits a blitzing defense is most vulnerable to, and the coach named them in the same week he said to expect blitzes.

And there’s a deeper version of it, which is the one I’d put to the coaching staff.

The argument for playing him is strongest in exactly the weeks you are least willing to.

If Dallas were five and three, a snap for an unknown tight end against a blitzing defense would be cheap. At three and five with a deadline on Tuesday, every snap feels expensive, which means the team’s willingness to develop a player falls precisely as its need for new answers rises.

That isn’t a failure of nerve. It’s a structural trap, and most teams in most seasons never escape it, which is why practice squad players tend to debut either in September or in January and almost never in November.
