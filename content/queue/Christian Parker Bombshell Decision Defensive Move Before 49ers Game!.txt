And the football logic is genuinely good, so let me build it.

An inside linebacker in space has to read, wait, and then commit. He’s responsible for a gap that might become a coverage assignment the moment a back releases, and the hardest thing in that job is not committing early.

An outside linebacker on the edge has one job on most snaps. Get off the ball and set the edge, or get off the ball and rush.

So the move isn’t about finding a better player. It’s about reducing the number of decisions.

Which the video puts better than I can. There might be a role that lets him attack instead of overthink.

That’s the sentence. And the general version is worth keeping, because it applies to far more than football.

When a man is good at the physical part and struggling with the reading part, you don’t coach him harder. You give him a job with fewer reads.

And his attributes fit it. The video describes an aggressive style, a man who wants to meet the blocker rather than wait, and physicality that shows up when he attacks the line of scrimmage.

Attacking the line is exactly what an edge defender does and exactly what an inside linebacker in coverage must not do. So the same instinct that makes him a liability in space makes him an asset on the edge.

Which is the cleanest version of a thing I’ve said about several players this month. A trait isn’t good or bad. It’s suited or unsuited, and the job is a variable.

Now San Francisco, because the opponent makes the experiment urgent and the mechanism is specific.

The video’s argument is that Kyle Shanahan doesn’t run an offense that lines up and lets you guess. The 49ers move people around, stretch you sideways, and make defenders question where the ball is going.

And the specific threat is outside zone with Christian McCaffrey, with an offensive line that works together, and the warning is that if the outside man gets caught inside, you give McCaffrey room to bounce it.

Let me explain why that’s the exact problem a new edge player would be walking into, because it’s the hardest assignment on a defense.

Outside zone is a run where the whole offensive line moves laterally and the back runs parallel to the line looking for a seam. It doesn’t attack a predetermined gap. It attacks whichever gap you open by chasing.

And the edge defender’s job against it is called setting the edge, which means refusing to be pushed inside, forcing the ball to stay between the tackles, and doing it while being blocked by a man moving sideways at speed.

Get it wrong and the run doesn’t gain four yards. It bounces outside into open grass.

So you have a rookie, in his first week with the outside group, against the offense in football that most punishes an edge defender for losing his outside leverage.

And that’s a genuine argument against doing it, and I’d want to be honest about it rather than only reporting the optimistic case.

The best week to try a man at a new position is not the week you play the team that attacks that position hardest.

But the video’s counter is also fair. Even for a few snaps, physicality that helps force plays back inside is worth exploring, and at least San Francisco would see something different.

Which is the real argument, and it isn’t about Barham. A defense with fifteen sacks and no new acquisitions has to show somebody something they haven’t prepared for, and a man who hasn’t played in a month is the one thing on film that doesn’t exist.

Growing while staying consistent is a precise description of a hard problem. A new coordinator has to improve his own calls and not change the language his players are learning, and those pull against each other. Every improvement to a system is a thing eleven men have to relearn.

Which is the install story I’ve been running since I found out Dallas changed six defensive starting spots in the offseason and hired a first-year coordinator. Now the thing in this video I most want to amplify, because it’s about expectations and it’s better than anything I’d have written.

If Barham gets a pressure on Sunday, that doesn’t make him the next Micah Parsons. And if he struggles with the new assignment, it doesn’t mean he can’t play in this league.

That is the correct posture and it is almost never taken, and the reason it’s correct is a sample size point.

A handful of snaps at a new position produces no information about a career. It produces one or two plays, and one or two plays are consistent with almost any underlying truth about a player.

So I’d put it as a rule, because it’s the same rule I’ve applied to a running back’s eleven carries and a receiver’s four targets this month.

The smaller the sample, the larger the claims people make from it.
