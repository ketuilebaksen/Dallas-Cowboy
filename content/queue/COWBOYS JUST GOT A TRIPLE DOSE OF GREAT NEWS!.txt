Alright. Now the substance, because there’s a genuinely good argument in here and it deserves better than its packaging.

Candace Buckner of The Athletic made the case that a victory can change a result without changing the larger concerns. Her position is that calling this a meaningful improvement would be generous, that Dallas still has hope in the NFC East, and that beyond that she sees a team that could be one and done in the playoffs.

And she put one statistic at the centre of it.

Four playoff wins in a quarter century.

Let me do the arithmetic on that, because it’s the only number in this video and nobody has turned it over.

A quarter century is twenty-five seasons. Four playoff wins across twenty-five seasons is one playoff win every six and a quarter years.

Now put that beside something I worked out four days ago from a different source. Since 1999, which is twenty-eight seasons, this is the twelfth Cowboys season with three or fewer wins through eight games. Twelve of twenty-eight is forty-three percent.

Those two figures are the same fact from two directions.

A franchise that starts forty-three percent of its seasons two games under water and wins four playoff games in twenty-five years is not having a bad year. It’s describing itself.

And here’s why that matters for the argument she’s actually making, and this is where I think her case is stronger than the way it’s being presented.

Four playoff wins in twenty-five years spans multiple head coaches. I don’t have the list and I won’t invent it, but twenty-five years is not one regime. It’s several. Which means the thing producing that number survived every coaching change inside it.

So when she points at Jerry Jones rather than at the coach, that isn’t a cheap shot. It’s the only inference the statistic supports.

Because the test for any explanation is whether it survives the thing it’s supposed to explain. Coaches came and went across those twenty-five years. The four playoff wins didn’t move. Therefore the coach is not the variable.

That’s a real argument and it’s the first time in three weeks anybody has pointed a criticism at a level of this organisation where the evidence actually points.

Now the part of her argument I think is weaker, and I’ll be fair about it.

She noted that Brian Schottenheimer is a first-time head coach and that he makes less money than many college quarterbacks.

Now, the salary comparison is a rhetorical device and it’s an effective one. It’s also not an argument, and I’d want to be careful with it for a specific reason.

A coach’s salary is evidence about what an organisation was willing to pay. It is not evidence about how well he coaches.

Those are two different claims and the second one does not follow from the first. Cheap coaches sometimes turn out to be excellent and expensive ones are frequently fired. If the argument is that Jerry Jones systematically undervalues coaching, the salary is a data point about Jones. If the argument is that Schottenheimer is bad at his job, the salary tells you nothing, because nobody’s play sheet improves when their contract does.

And the video, to its credit, draws exactly that line. It says her point is not simply that a different coach would guarantee a win, that the source does not establish that, and that neither does her criticism.

Now the thing I want to add, because it’s the measurement question nobody asks and it’s the heart of today.

What would a convincing win have looked like on Sunday?

Because the criticism is that beating Indianapolis by four doesn’t prove anything, and the specific reason given is that the Colts were described as being in the midst of a fire sale before the trade deadline.

Fine. Take that seriously. But then say what the alternative was.

Dallas was on the road, against a team that had just been selling, having lost a fourteen-point fourth quarter lead the week before, with its starting tight end questionable, its best receiver on a month-old hamstring, its number one corner on an ankle he’s managed since week four, an inactive leader in sweats, and two safeties who only cleared concussion protocol on Friday.

And they won by four, having trailed inside two minutes.

So here’s the question I’d put to anybody who found that unpersuasive.

If that team wins by twenty, what have you learned that you didn’t learn by them winning by four?

Because a margin is mostly a measure of the opponent and the variance, and a one-score road win against a bad team is the single most common result in this sport. It’s what good teams and mediocre teams both produce most weeks.

Which gives you the principle, and it’s the one I’d want you to leave with.

A result can only tell you what it was designed to measure.

Sunday was a test of whether Dallas could respond after a mistake. It was not a test of whether Dallas is good, because nothing involving a two-and-six team can be.

And on the question it did test, it answered. Trailing, under two minutes, Prescott drove the field and Javonte Williams scored. Then the defense got the stop and Caleb Downs intercepted it. The week before, in the identical situation, Dallas lost in overtime.

Same mistake. Different reaction. That’s what was being measured and that’s what resolved.

That is an excellent sentence and it’s rare. Nobody in this exchange is disputing a fact. Dallas trailed inside two minutes, Prescott led the winning drive, the final score was twenty-seven to twenty-three. All agreed. The disagreement is entirely about what that licenses you to believe.

And that distinction has a name worth having.

When two people agree on the facts and disagree on the conclusion, the dispute is about the standard, not the evidence.

Buckner’s standard is the playoffs. By that standard a four-point road win over a selling team is close to meaningless, and she’s right.

Jon Breech’s standard is the week. By that standard this was a game Dallas could not afford to lose and they did just enough, and he’s right too, and he gave them a passing grade.

Neither of them is wrong. They’re grading different exams.

My standard was two consecutive wins. Now the thing I want to push back on hardest, because it’s the most common error in football commentary and this video avoids it while quoting people who don’t.

A comeback win and a convincing statement are not automatically the same thing.

Correct. But the inverse is just as true and almost nobody says it.

A close game is not evidence of weakness.

Most games in this league are close. That’s the design. The schedule, the draft, the salary cap and the rules all exist to compress the distance between the best team and the worst, and they work. Blowouts are the exception and they are usually about variance rather than quality.

So when a critic says Dallas needed a late touchdown to beat a bad team, the honest response is that most teams need a late touchdown to beat most teams, and the ones that don’t were usually lucky rather than superior.

Which is why the fire sale point, while fair, proves less than it appears. A team selling before a deadline is worse on paper and still has eleven professionals on the field for sixty minutes, in their own stadium, at one o’clock.

Now, where I think Buckner lands the hardest blow, and I don’t think anybody has noticed it.

Her phrase is that Dallas still has hope of competing in the NFC East, and that beyond that she sees a team that could be one and done.

That’s not pessimism. That’s an accurate description of the ceiling implied by the numbers I’ve been carrying for a fortnight.

Dallas is seventh in the league in scoring and twenty-seventh in points allowed. Thirtieth in takeaways. Twenty-fourth in sacks. Four and five, two games back in the loss column.

A team built like that can absolutely win a division, because divisions are won by four or five good weeks. And a team built like that is a very hard team to win a January road game with, because January removes the weak opponents from the schedule and a defense ranked twenty-seventh has nowhere to hide.

So one and done is not a slur. It’s what the ranking distribution predicts, and the honest version of the optimistic case is not that she’s wrong. It’s that making the playoffs at all would represent a genuine recovery from three and five, and that what happens after that is a separate question nobody can answer in November.

What I want you to take out of here is the standards point, because it will save you an enormous amount of pointless arguing.

When two people agree on the facts and disagree on the conclusion, the dispute is about the standard, not the evidence.
