Let me explain what a halftime adjustment actually is, because people imagine a coach drawing up brand new plays in the locker room, and that's not how it works.

During the first half, coaches in the booth are charting everything. What formations the offense is using, what plays come out of them, which defensive calls are getting beaten and why. By halftime, the staff has a picture of the other team's plan for that specific day.

Then in about twelve minutes, the coordinator makes a handful of targeted changes. Maybe he stops running a coverage that's getting exploited. Maybe he changes who's responsible for the quarterback on a certain look. Maybe he simplifies, taking away calls that are causing confusion.

The key is that it has to be small enough for the players to absorb in twelve minutes and execute immediately. Great halftime adjustments are usually boring. And holding a mobile quarterback to ten points in a half suggests Parker made a few good boring ones.

And remember what we said about this scheme. Christian Parker wants to stop the run with lighter boxes, meaning fewer defenders near the line and more in coverage. That only works if the defensive line wins up front. Two weeks in, the evidence says it isn't winning consistently enough.

And there's a piece of math here that explains why running quarterbacks make this so much harder, and it's the most important concept for Sunday.

Run defense is fundamentally a counting game. Every run play has a certain number of gaps, the spaces between blockers where a ball carrier can go. The defense needs one player responsible for every gap. If there are more gaps than defenders, somebody runs free.

Against a normal quarterback on a normal handoff, the quarterback isn't a runner. He hands the ball off and becomes a spectator. The defense only has to account for the running back.

But when the quarterback is a genuine running threat, he's an extra ball carrier. That effectively adds one more gap the defense has to account for on every read play. Coaches call it plus one. The offense gains a man, and the defense either has to bring an extra defender into the box to match it, or it's short.

And that's the tension with Parker's lighter boxes. If you keep extra defenders in coverage, you're already a man light against the run. Add a running quarterback, and you're effectively two men light, unless your defensive linemen win their one-on-ones and take away gaps by themselves.

That's why the defensive line matters so much this week. Against Lamar Jackson, every defensive tackle who gets moved off the ball doesn't just lose a rep. He opens a gap the scheme was counting on him to close, and there's nobody behind him to fix it.
