Now the ninety-eight point six percent, because this is where everybody is going to reason badly today and I’d rather you didn’t.

With five minutes left, ESPN’s model gave Dallas a ninety-eight point six percent chance to win. Dallas lost.

The first thing to understand is that this is not evidence the model was wrong.

A model that says ninety-eight point six percent is making a claim about frequency. It’s saying that out of every seventy-one times a team is in this situation, it wins seventy and loses one. If a model said ninety-eight point six percent and the team always won, the model would be badly calibrated. It would be understating itself.

So the one point four percent is not a glitch. It’s the product. A probability that is never wrong is not a probability.

And across a league of thirty-two teams playing sixteen or seventeen games, there are hundreds of late-game situations every season that sit at or above ninety-eight percent. One point four percent of hundreds is several. Somebody loses one of these every few weeks, and this week it was Dallas.

Now the second thing, which is the opposite of the first, and both are true.

A probability is a statement about the situation, not about the participants.

The model does not know who is on the field. It does not know that two of three interior defensive linemen were on the injury report. It does not know whether a team has a pattern of failing to close. It takes a score, a time, a field position and a possession, and it tells you what has historically happened to the average team in that spot.

And that’s the honest bridge between the model and the criticism, and I haven’t heard anybody make it.

If this team is systematically worse at closing games than the average team, then its true probability in that situation was never one point four percent. The one point four is a population average. A team drawn from the wrong end of the population isn’t described by it.

So the correct reading of today is not the model was wrong and it’s not we were unlucky. It’s that a population statistic was applied to a specific team, as it always is, and the specific team may not belong to the population.

Which is a testable claim, and I’ll tell you what would test it. Not this game. You’d need the record of this team’s fourth-quarter leads over a meaningful stretch and a comparison to the league.
