Here’s tell number ten, and write this one down, because you’ll use it every week for the rest of your life.

If a headline tells you what somebody said, the body has to contain quotation marks. No quotation marks means no statement.

And let me explain why this particular trick is the most common one in all of sports media, because the reason is almost beautiful in its cynicism.

A statement is the only kind of news you can promise without having any.

Think about it. If you claim a trade happened, somebody can check the transaction wire. If you claim an injury, there’s an official report. If you claim a signing, there’s a contract. Those are events in the world and the world keeps records of them.

But a quote? A quote just requires a person to have a mouth. You can promise one in a title, deliver nine minutes of speculation, and nobody can prove a negative. There’s no quote registry. There’s no central database of things athletes didn’t say.

So you won’t believe what he said is the cheapest sentence in media, because what he said can be absolutely nothing and the title still gets clicked.

And notice how this differs from tell number nine, which I gave you yesterday. Tell nine was the title doesn’t match the video, and my example was a title about a running back attached to a video about an edge rusher. That’s a mismatch.

Tell ten is worse. Tell ten invents an event. It doesn’t point at the wrong thing in the video. It points at something that never happened anywhere.

So here’s the two-second test, and it costs you nothing. Before you watch a video promising you what somebody said, ask yourself what the actual sentence is supposed to be. If nobody can tell you the words, there weren’t any.

Alright. Now the part I actually care about. Should Dallas want this?

And my answer is no, and the reason is an argument I don’t think I’ve heard anybody make, so let me build it properly.

I call it the backup quarterback paradox.

A backup quarterback can have two possible jobs. And they are mutually exclusive.

Job number one is to be ready to win football games you would otherwise lose. That requires a veteran who knows the system cold, can run the existing offense with no modifications, needs zero developmental snaps, and can walk onto the field in the second quarter of a road game and execute a call sheet he’s seen a thousand times. His value is measured in games preserved.

Job number two is to develop into a future starter. That requires practice repetitions, first-team work, meaningful snaps in real games, and an organization willing to accept some losing now in exchange for a lot of winning later. His value is measured in years.

Now here’s the paradox. A contending team cannot pay for job number two.

Because the currency of development is snaps, and those snaps belong to the starter. There is exactly one football and one first-team offense, and a healthy franchise quarterback takes essentially all of it.

So a high-upside young quarterback on a team with a healthy starter gets neither thing. He doesn’t develop, because he never plays. And he isn’t insurance, because insurance requires total mastery of the current offense, which is precisely the thing a young developmental quarterback hasn’t had time to acquire.

He becomes the most expensive bench seat in professional sports. And every snap he takes in practice to develop is a snap stolen from preparing the team that’s actually playing on Sunday.
