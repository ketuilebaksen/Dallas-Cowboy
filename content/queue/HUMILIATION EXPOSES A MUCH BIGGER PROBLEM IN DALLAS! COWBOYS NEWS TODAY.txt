Which means the twenty-one points in the fourth quarter are the baseline, not the achievement. The nineteen through three quarters is the number that measures this offense against a fresh defense.

Because a defense in the fourth quarter has run a hundred and twenty yards of pursuit and absorbed sixty collisions. The offense’s job is to score before that discount arrives, because you only get the discount if the game is still alive when it does.

Now the central claim, and this is the one that deserves the most scrutiny.

The team is regressing while the talent is increasing. If your roster has gotten better but the results have gotten worse, who’s in charge of managing the pieces on the roster?

His answer is the head coach.

Now that is a real argument and I’ll steelman it before I complicate it.

The steelman. Dallas added two first round picks and Kenny Clark for Micah Parsons. It spent five of seven draft picks on defense including two first rounders. It traded for Joey Porter Junior and for a linebacker from San Francisco. Quinnen Williams is reportedly leading the National Football League in tackles for loss. The players themselves say it’s the best roster in years.

And the team is last in the league in total defense, thirtieth in scoring, four and six, and one and four at home.

So inputs up, outputs down, and the man who converts inputs into outputs is the head coach.

Now the complication, and it’s the thing I’ve been saying all month.

Talent and a roster are not the same object.

Dallas changed six defensive starting spots in one offseason. It installed a new scheme under a first-year coordinator. It has two first-round rookies in that unit, one of whom is on injured reserve.

So the talent went up and the continuity went down, at the same time, by the same decisions.

And a defense is the side of the ball where continuity matters most, because eleven men have to act as one object and that only comes from repetition. A defense wins by making one thing look like two things, and that requires every man to know what the other ten are doing without being told.

So I’d put it this way. The roster didn’t get better. The talent got better and the roster got newer, and those have opposite effects on a defense in year one.

Which doesn’t exonerate the head coach. It just means the question isn’t who’s managing the pieces. It’s who chose to replace that many pieces at once, and that’s the general manager.

And NBC Sports published exactly that framing yesterday, attributing the defensive difficulties to Jerry Jones.

Now his strongest argument, saved for last, and I think it’s the best thing in the video.

He said he wouldn’t even care if the next head coach wanted to keep Christian Parker, because the real problem is higher up.

And here’s the reasoning.

The defense has looked historically bad with two completely different coordinators, two nearly different systems, and two entirely different groups of players. If that’s true, the common factor isn’t the coordinator, it’s the head coach.

Now that’s a proper piece of logic and it deserves a name, because it’s a method rather than an opinion.

Tell fifty-seven. Change the variables and find what didn’t change.

If you swap the coordinator, swap the scheme and swap the players, and the output is the same, then the cause is in what you didn’t swap.

That’s how you isolate a cause in any system, and it’s the most rigorous argument anybody has made about this football team.

And I want to test it honestly, because there are two things to check.

First, is the premise true? Two coordinators, two systems, two groups of players, both historically bad.

Last season Dallas finished seven, nine and one and missed the playoffs. I derived that figure weeks ago by subtracting the current record from the career record, and it’s been confirmed. So the previous season was bad.

So the premise holds, if both defenses were genuinely bad, and I’d want the previous season’s defensive ranking before I fully accept it. I don’t have it. If you do, tell me below, because this argument lives or dies on it.

Second, and this is the thing he misses. If you change the variables and find what didn’t change, you have to list everything that didn’t change.

The head coach didn’t change. The general manager didn’t change. The owner didn’t change. They’re the same person for two of those three.

So the common factor isn’t a factor. It’s at least two, and one of them has held the job since 1989.

Which means his method is right and his conclusion skips a step, and he should be arguing about both men rather than one.

Now the time argument, which the transcript cuts off mid-sentence and which I’ll complete because I know where it goes.

If the head coach’s attention is the problem, the unit he’s attending to should be the better one. It is. That’s consistent with his argument.

Here’s where we are.

Dallas is four and six, last in the league in total defense, thirtieth in scoring at twenty-eight point two a game, and one and four at home. Mike Pettine is reportedly taking over defensive play calling with Christian Parker remaining coordinator. Tennessee is at home on Sunday.

And today’s argument, which I’d summarise fairly as this. The talent gap with San Francisco isn’t large, the front office advantage isn’t there, the defense has been bad under two coordinators with two systems and two groups of players, and the common factor is the head coach.

Three things I’d say to that argument.

One. The symmetry doesn’t work. San Francisco’s forty included twenty-one points conceded while leading by twenty-nine, and Dallas’s forty-six were conceded in a competitive game.

Two. The method is right and the conclusion is incomplete. Change the variables and find what didn’t change is exactly how you isolate a cause, and what didn’t change includes the general manager.

Three. The best roster in years and the newest roster in years are the same roster, and on defense those pull in opposite directions.

And the thing he’s right about, which I won’t dilute. Twenty-nine points allowed in a second half, three touchdowns of sixty-five yards or more with nobody touching the scorer, a cover zero with Von Miller on Christian McCaffrey, and nineteen points scored in three quarters. Somebody is responsible for all four of those, and the players aren’t choosing them.

What I want you to take out of here is tell fifty-seven, because it’s the most useful thinking tool anybody in this genre has handed me in six weeks.

Change the variables and find what didn’t change.

If the coordinator changes and the scheme changes and the players change and the result doesn’t, stop looking at the things that changed.

That’s not a football idea. That’s how you find a cause in anything, and the only correction I’d make is that you have to list everything that stayed the same, not just the one that suits your conclusion.
