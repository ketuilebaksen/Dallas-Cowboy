Let’s get into it.

Let’s start with the answer itself, because the structure of it tells you how this coaching staff thinks.

Everybody wanted to know how Dallas is going to get after the quarterback more consistently. More sacks. More pressure. Something.

And the head coach began with coverage.

His point was that if the quarterback immediately sees an open receiver, the football is gone before your edge rusher can possibly get there. You can bring extra pressure, you can mix things up, but none of it matters if the ball leaves in two seconds.

And honestly, that’s correct, and it’s the part fans systematically misunderstand.

Here’s the mechanism, and it’s the most important idea in this video.

A pass rush does not have a fixed amount of time. It has whatever time the coverage gives it.

The average NFL quarterback gets the ball out in somewhere around two and a half seconds when he knows where he’s going. An elite edge rusher, winning his matchup cleanly, needs roughly three and a half to four seconds to reach the quarterback.

So if your coverage leaves a man open on his first read, the rush is mathematically irrelevant. It isn’t that your rushers are slow. It’s that nobody on earth gets there in two and a half seconds.

Now flip it. If the coverage is tight and the quarterback has to work to his second and third options, that same rusher suddenly has four seconds, and now he’s a monster.

Same player. Same effort. Completely different outcome, determined entirely by somebody else’s job.

That’s why you cannot evaluate a pass rush in isolation, and it’s why a hundred thirty passer rating is a coverage indictment before it’s a pass rush indictment.

Now, I want to be honest about how this interacts with something I told you two days ago, because he’s partly confirming me and partly correcting me.

Two days ago I made the Von Miller argument. Sixty-two snaps in four games. And I told you his snap count was a symptom rather than a cause, because a pass rush specialist only comes onto the field when a pass is near-certain, which means his playing time is downstream of your early-down defense. I said the real question wasn’t why isn’t Von Miller playing more, it was why isn’t this defense creating third and long.

Schottenheimer is making the same shape of argument and pointing at a different cause. He’s saying the rush is downstream of coverage.

So which is it?

Both, and they aren’t competing. They’re two different clocks.

My argument was about snaps. How many opportunities a rusher gets, which is determined by down and distance. His argument is about seconds. How long a rusher has on each of those opportunities, which is determined by coverage.

Put them together and you have the full picture. Early-down defense determines how often your rushers are on the field in useful situations. Coverage determines how long they have once they’re there.

And Dallas has been failing at both. That’s four sacks in four games. Not a talent problem. A structural one, from two directions.

Now, the part of his answer I think is most reassuring. He still believes the edge talent is there.

And he singled out Quinnen Williams for an outstanding game against Houston.

Regular viewers know I argued in September that Williams was being misused as a read-and-react player when his whole game is penetrating as a one-gap three-technique. He’s now been good for two consecutive weeks and his head coach is praising him publicly. I’m not going to take a lap. The fix appears to have happened, and that’s enough.

And the plan Schottenheimer described is exactly the right one. Push the pocket from the inside, make the quarterback uncomfortable, and let the outside rush finish.

That’s the architecture of a good pass rush. Interior pressure takes away the quarterback’s feet, because there’s nowhere to step up into. Edge pressure takes away his escape. Neither works alone. A quarterback with a collapsing interior but open edges just runs sideways. A quarterback with speed on the edges and a clean middle just steps up into a comfortable pocket.

Those pieces have to work together, and that phrase is going to come back in a minute.

Now here’s the coaching instruction that made me sit up, because it explains the single worst pass rush I have criticized all season.

Schottenheimer said Dallas cannot get so caught up chasing sacks that everybody runs past the play.

And then he gave the specific technique. Once an edge rusher reaches the quarterback’s depth, he has to turn inward and squeeze the pocket. Close that opening.

Because otherwise the quarterback simply steps forward into open grass, and your pressure has turned into a first down.

Now go back to the Baltimore game with me.

I told you that on Baltimore’s last touchdown pass, Rashan Gary ran at Lamar Jackson with his hands in the air the entire way, and I said that’s a technique for rushing a statue.

That’s this error. That’s exactly this error, described by the head coach in his own words.

A rusher who runs to the quarterback’s depth and keeps going, arms up, has vacated the very space he was supposed to be occupying. He’s created the lane he was sent to close.

And here’s the mechanism nobody explains properly.

The goal of a pass rush is not to touch the quarterback. The goal is to shrink the space he’s standing in.

An edge rusher who gets upfield fast and stays upfield has actually widened the pocket. He’s now behind the quarterback, which makes him a spectator, and the quarterback has a clean sidewalk in front of him.

An edge rusher who gets to that depth and then turns in has made the pocket smaller. Even if he never makes the tackle, the quarterback has nowhere comfortable to put his feet, and his throw gets worse.

Pass rushing is architecture, not a race.

And the fact that the head coach said this out loud, unprompted, in a press conference, tells you it’s being coached this week. That’s the difference between a staff that noticed and a staff that didn’t.

Now the second half of that thought, because he connected it back to coverage in a way I found genuinely sharp.

In match coverage, defenders are following receivers. Which means they are not watching the quarterback.

So when the quarterback takes off, a linebacker has to decide. Does he come forward to meet the run, or stay with his assignment? React too early and the quarterback throws the ball behind him into the space he just left.

That’s an impossible choice, and it’s created by somebody else’s failure. If the pocket had been controlled, that linebacker never has to guess.

So Schottenheimer’s instruction to his front is essentially this. Stop putting the guys behind you in that position.

And I love that framing, because it makes the pass rush responsible for the coverage in the same breath that he made the coverage responsible for the pass rush. That’s a coach telling his defense there are no individual failures on that side of the football. Everything is somebody else’s time, space, or decision.

Now, the secondary, and this is the part of the press conference nobody has reported properly. It’s the biggest tactical news of the week.

The arrival of Joey Porter Junior has changed the assignments.

Porter is working on the boundary. And DaRon Bland has moved toward the field side.

Let me explain what that means, because the terms are simple once you hear them.

A football field isn’t symmetrical relative to where the ball is. The ball sits somewhere between the hash marks, which means one sideline is closer than the other.

The boundary is the short side. The field side is the wide side, where there’s more grass.

Those are genuinely different jobs. A boundary cornerback has the sideline helping him, acting as a twelfth defender, so he can play more aggressively and press. A field-side cornerback has far more space to defend, which means he needs more range and more discipline, and he gets less help from the geometry.

And here’s the strategic choice underneath it. Dallas prefers side-based assignments rather than having one cornerback follow a receiver everywhere he goes.

Side-based is simpler. Each corner learns one side, one set of footwork, one relationship with his safety. Less thinking, fewer busts, faster play. That’s the simplify philosophy we’ve been tracking all month, applied to personnel. The cost is that the offense gets to choose the matchups. If they want their best receiver on your weaker corner, they just line him up over there.

Travel assignments are the opposite. You control the matchup, but one defender is now playing both sides, in both alignments, with help arriving from different directions. That’s a lot of mental load.

And now watch how that explains the thing that beat Dallas on Sunday.

Houston put Nico Collins in the slot immediately. And Dallas wasn’t expecting quite that much movement from him.

So if your assignments are side-based, and the other team takes its best receiver and moves him inside, you’ve just handed your best cornerback nobody to cover while your nickel defender gets the problem.

That’s not a coverage bust. That’s a structural mismatch created by formation, and it’s exactly the same trick Dallas itself used in the second half of that game when it put CeeDee Lamb and George Pickens on the same side.

Both teams were playing the same game. Houston just did it first.

Porter did handle some slot assignments, so there’s flexibility there. The coach’s point was that everybody needs to understand the adjustments.

And the obvious follow-up question got asked. Could Porter eventually travel with a number one receiver?

Schottenheimer said the possibility exists. But he just arrived, and you cannot overload somebody who’s still learning and then expect him to play without hesitation.

That’s exactly right, and it’s the same point I made about the install problem when he was acquired. Defense is a language. A man translating in his head plays slower than a man who knows the words.

This week he has more meetings and more preparation behind him. And the coach sounded genuinely impressed by three specific things. His quiet approach. His competitive practices. And his ability to jump straight into meaningful snaps.

Note what’s missing from that list. Nobody is talking about his talent, because the talent was never in question. They’re talking about his professionalism, which is what you say about a man who’s integrating well.

Now, that’s a compliment and a problem in the same sentence, and I want to be fair about both.

The compliment is real. Recognition is the hardest part of playing cornerback. Technique can be drilled. Speed is given. But seeing a formation, reading a route combination and knowing what’s coming is pattern recognition built from repetition.

The problem is the timing. Dallas needs that recognition in the first quarter, not after spending half a football game finding its footing.

And there’s a structural reason it took that long, which connects to everything we just discussed. If you’ve just changed a man’s assignment from one side of the field to the other, in a week when a new cornerback arrived and the call sheet got simplified, then of course recognition comes slower. Give up a play, move on, get ready for the next one.

That matters more at cornerback than anywhere else in football, because a cornerback is the only defender who loses in public and alone. Everybody in the stadium knows whose fault it was, and there’s no hiding, because the next snap is coming in forty seconds and it might be at you again.

A corner who carries the last play into the next one gets beaten twice. The ability to forget isn’t a personality trait at that position. It’s a professional skill.

Now, Jaishawn Barham, because the coaching language here is precise and worth decoding.

His downhill aggression is a strength. Keep that. Nobody wants to coach that out of him.

But the coverage responsibilities are new. And the specific job Schottenheimer described is this. He has to count the receiving threats from the outside inward, identify his assignment, and react correctly.

Let me explain what counting from outside in actually means, because it’s a genuinely elegant piece of football.

In zone and match coverage, defenders don’t memorize names. They number receivers by their position in the formation, starting from the sideline and working toward the ball. The outermost man is number one, the next one inside him is number two, and so on.

Why do it that way? Because formations change constantly, and you can’t memorize every combination. But a rule that says you take number two to your side works no matter who lines up there.

So on every single snap, a linebacker has to look at the formation, count, find his man, and then react to the route. In about two seconds. Before the ball moves.

And Schottenheimer said he sees improvement in Barham’s eyes, which is coach-speak for his processing is getting faster.

But then the honest part. Play-action can still pull him forward.

Now, I flagged exactly this weeks ago, and I want to be careful not to sound like I’m gloating, because this is the most predictable problem in football.

Play-action works by making a linebacker take a false step. And the linebackers who are most vulnerable to it are the aggressive, instinctive, downhill ones, because their first instinct is to attack what they see. The very quality that makes Barham special at stopping Derrick Henry on fourth down is the quality that makes him bite on a fake.

That’s the balance, and it’s the hardest thing in the world to coach. Stay aggressive, while recognizing when the offense is trying to use your aggression against you.

You don’t fix that with a meeting. You fix it with about forty games.

Now, why does protection matter so much this particular week? Because of who’s calling Tampa Bay’s defense.

Todd Bowles. And Schottenheimer expects the identity he’s seen throughout Bowles’s career.

Here’s the number. Tampa Bay blitzed on thirty-five percent of their pass rush snaps last season. Third highest in the entire NFL.

Thirty-five percent. More than one in three.

And Schottenheimer’s warning was specific. They may have pressured less in their previous game. Don’t assume the blitzes aren’t coming. Prepare for them.

That’s exactly the right instruction, and here’s the mechanism. A blitz-heavy coordinator doesn’t blitz every week, he blitzes when the matchup invites it. One quiet game tells you nothing about the plan for the next one, because the plan is built against you, not against the last opponent.

They do have young players at linebacker, at nickel, and on the edge. Which is relevant, because blitzing with inexperienced players is high-variance. It produces both free runners and blown assignments.

And the instruction Schottenheimer gave is the best protection advice in football. Know where the answers are before the snap, instead of trying to figure everything out once somebody’s already coming free.

That’s what protection really is. Not strength. Pre-snap arithmetic.

Now, the tool he described for handling it, and it’s elegant.

The tight ends.

Dallas trusts their football intelligence enough to shift them, change blocking angles, and use chips. And a quick motion can prevent the defense from resetting its front, or put unexpected contact on an edge defender.

Let me explain why that works. When a tight end motions late, the defense has a fraction of a second to re-identify the strength of the formation and adjust. Against a blitz-heavy team, that fraction of a second is the difference between a designed pressure and a scrambled one, because a blitz is a timed, coordinated thing and late motion ruins the timing.

And a chip is simply a brief, unexpected hit on a rusher as he comes off the line. It doesn’t block him. It just knocks his path and his timing off by a half-second, which is often all a tackle needs.

Those details matter against a Tampa Bay edge group that includes Yaya Diaby.

Now, a small organizational detail I found genuinely interesting, because it’s about how a head coach survives calling plays.

Schottenheimer prepares fourth down plans during the week, with different approaches depending on field position. During games, Ryan Feder gives recommendations and helps keep that process organized. And the coach makes the final decision.

That structure matters more than it sounds, because it goes straight to the thing that cost Dallas a football game in Rio.

A head coach who also calls the offense has two jobs competing at the same time. Play calling is immediate and absorbing. You’re looking at a sheet, watching personnel, thinking about the next three snaps. Game management is the opposite. Clock, timeouts, field position, fourth down, challenges. It requires you to be looking up, not down.

And a man cannot do both perfectly in the same moment. That’s not a criticism, it’s a physical limitation.

Which is exactly what failed in Rio. A timeout spent and then not used. A touchback with seven seconds left. Those are management failures, not play-calling failures, and they happened to a coach who was also calling an offense.

So having somebody whose job is to hold the fourth down plan and keep the process organized, while the head coach stays in the play sheet, is the correct structural answer to a problem that genuinely cost this team a game. Calling plays cannot mean losing track of the clock.
