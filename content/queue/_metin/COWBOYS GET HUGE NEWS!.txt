And now look at what this video says about how Dallas can clear a second roster spot, because it lists three methods and it’s a complete list. Teams usually clear a spot one of three ways. They waive or release somebody. They move an injured player to injured reserve. Or they trade a player away.

Here’s the principle underneath that, and I think it’s one of the better ideas I’ve had this month.

Versatility is an asset when you choose it and a liability when it’s forced.

A player who can play three positions is valuable because it gives a coordinator options. He’s a solution to a problem that hasn’t happened yet.

A player who must play three positions because of injuries is being asked to master three jobs at once, which means mastering none of them. The same flexibility that made him valuable is now the reason he never settles anywhere.

And the rookie part makes it considerably worse, because of something I’ve said repeatedly about young defenders.

A rookie’s problem is recognition. And recognition is position-specific.

Slot corner recognition is reading a receiver’s release and the leverage of a route combination from two yards away. Box recognition is reading a guard’s first step and fitting a run. Free safety recognition is reading a quarterback’s eyes and the depth of three routes from fourteen yards away.

Those are three different skills that share no actual content. Which means a rookie bouncing between three spots is accumulating experience at roughly a third of the rate he would at one, in the only skill that determines whether he becomes good.

And now look at the number. Ninety-four yards on seven catches is thirteen point four yards per reception.

That’s not a quickness problem. Thirteen and a half yards a catch is intermediate and deep, which is depth judgment and angle discipline, which is exactly what a slot corner playing free safety would be worst at. A slot corner’s instincts are built for short, quick, horizontal things. Free safety is long, slow, vertical, and about managing space behind you rather than in front of you.

So the ninety-four yards is not evidence that this rookie is bad. It’s evidence that he was playing a position he isn’t trained for, and the shape of the damage tells you so.

And the video attaches the right caveat, which is that it wouldn’t hang too much on one game. Correct. One game, seven targets.
