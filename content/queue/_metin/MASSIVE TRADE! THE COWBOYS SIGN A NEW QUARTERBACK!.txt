The headline names the wrong transaction.

Trade, signing, extension, release, waiver claim, elevation, promotion. In casual conversation those sound like shades of the same thing, moves, business, roster stuff. In the actual operation of a football team they are completely different legal events with completely different prices, and swapping one for another is not exaggeration. It’s a description of a different event entirely.

A trade means assets moved in both directions. Somebody gave up a pick, or a player, or took on money, and both teams agreed, and the league office processed it, and something is permanently gone from one side of the ledger.

A practice squad signing means a man who was unemployed on Monday has a job on Tuesday. No pick. No cap charge of any consequence. No roster spot. No other team’s permission. And critically, no exclusivity, which I’ll come back to, because that’s the part that bit Dallas last week.

So the test for tell seventeen is one sentence long.

If the answer is nothing, it wasn’t a trade. Here is the ladder, in order of what it costs you.

At the top, the fifty-three man active roster. Every spot on it is zero-sum, which I’ve said all season. Every addition is a subtraction. To sign a man to the fifty-three you must release or move somebody else, which means the price of the cheapest free agent in America is never zero. It is always one roster spot, which is the scarcest thing a football team owns.

Below that, injured reserve and the various designations, which buy you a spot at the cost of a four-game minimum absence.

Below that, the practice squad. Seventeen players, separate from the fifty-three, paid considerably less, in the building every single day, in meetings, in practice, and available to be promoted the moment somebody gets hurt.

For years, teams carried two quarterbacks and prayed. Then the league created a provision for an emergency third quarterback, so that a team doesn’t lose a game to a position nobody can fake. The practical effect of the rule is this. You don’t have to burn one of your fifty-three roster spots carrying a third quarterback all season long, as long as you have one in the building, developed in your system, who can be designated and made available when you need him.

In the building is the operative phrase. The rule turns the practice squad quarterback from a spare body into functional insurance.

And this is where a framework I built earlier in the season actually pays off, so let me apply it, because I think it’s the single most useful way to think about any backup in football.

I said a few weeks ago that insurance is overpriced at running back and underpriced at offensive tackle, and the reason is the consequence of failure. If your starting running back goes down, the next man gets the carries and your offense is somewhat worse. If your left tackle goes down, your quarterback gets hit from behind on a blind side and your season can end on one play. Same injury, wildly different consequence, and the market prices them almost the same.

Quarterback three is the purest case of that logic in all of football.

The probability you need him is low. The consequence if you need him and don’t have him is catastrophic, because there is no position on the field where a body off the street is less able to function. You cannot put a receiver back there. You cannot have a running back take snaps for a quarter in a league this fast.

And the price of the insurance is close to zero.

Low probability, catastrophic consequence, nearly free premium. You buy that every single time. Which means the correct lesson from the Milton situation is not that Dallas blundered. Which brings me to the question the video dances around for ten minutes without ever asking directly.

How is a Heisman Trophy finalist on a practice squad?

And the answer is one of the most important facts in football, so let me give it to you properly.

Quarterback is the position where college production translates worst. Not badly. Worst. Worse than every other position on the field, by a wide margin.

And there are specific reasons, so let me give you four.

First, coverage. Major college defenses, even very good ones, play more single-high safety and more soft zone than National Football League defenses do. The windows are wider. A throw that is open in the Southeastern Conference is a throw that gets intercepted on a Sunday, because the professional version of that same coverage has a defender sitting in a spot the college version leaves empty.

Second, speed at the second level. Linebackers and safeties in the National Football League close distance at a rate that invalidates a huge category of college throws, the ones where the receiver is open at the moment of the decision. In the professional game you don’t throw to where he is, you throw to where he’s about to be, against defenders who are also throwing their bodies to where he’s about to be.

Third, pre-snap. College offenses frequently simplify the quarterback’s job on purpose. Read one side, read one defender, get it out. The professional game asks a man to identify the front, the coverage shell, the likely rotation after the snap, the pressure, and the protection adjustment, in about four seconds, against a defense actively lying to him about all five.

And fourth, and this is the one almost nobody says out loud. In college, a great team has a talent advantage. Florida in 2020 could beat a lot of opponents by simply being better, and a quarterback operating behind a talent advantage gets cleaner pockets, more open receivers, and more margin for a late decision.

In the National Football League, nobody has a talent advantage. Every defense you face is made of professionals. There is no soft week. And when the margin disappears, what’s left is the precise set of skills that margin was hiding.

So a Heisman finalist on a practice squad is not usually a story about a fraud or a bust. It’s a story about the hardest translation in sports, and the fact that production is a joint achievement of the player and the circumstances, and only one of those two comes with you to the next level.

And there’s one more thing I want to add, because I built a framework for this a few days ago and today is the inverted case.

I talked about the backup quarterback paradox when we were discussing Shedeur Sanders. The argument was that the backup quarterback job contains two mutually exclusive tasks. Be ready to win games right now, and develop into a future starter. And those conflict, because development requires snaps, and a contending team cannot spend snaps, so a contender literally cannot pay the price of developing a quarterback.

Quarterback three is the clean version of that, because it only has one job.

The third quarterback is not a development project. He is an availability asset. His entire function is to know the offense well enough to run it competently on four hours notice, and the skills that serve that job are mental, not physical. Command of the install. Protection rules. Where the checkdown lives. What the call means when the play clock is at six.

Which, incidentally, is a job a twenty-eight year old who has spent five years in professional meeting rooms is far better suited to than a rookie. So if you’re wondering why teams keep signing veteran practice squad quarterbacks instead of developing young ones, that’s why. The job description rewards experience and does not reward upside.
