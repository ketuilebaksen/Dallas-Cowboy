So the diagnostic, and it’s the simplest one left.

When a story exists to debunk a rumour, check whether the headline is repeating the rumour.

Because that’s the trick, and it’s more elegant than fabrication. You don’t have to invent anything. You take a piece of reporting that says the thing didn’t happen, and you put the thing at the top of it. The people who read the body learn the truth. The people who read the headline learn the rumour, and they are the larger group by a wide margin.

Which means a debunking can propagate the thing it debunks, and the mechanism is the headline.

A hundred and forty-two million over four years is thirty-five and a half million a year.

And he is currently on twenty-seven. So the projection is about eight and a half million a year above what he’s being paid now, which tells you the market thinks he has outperformed a franchise tag.

And now the comparison that matters, because a second tag is the obvious alternative and the mechanics of it are brutal.

A franchise tag is not the same number twice. Repeat tags escalate, by rule, by a substantial percentage. Which means a 2027 tag on this man would land well above twenty-seven million for a single year, with no guaranteed money beyond it, and then you’d be in the identical position again twelve months later with a more expensive starting point.

So the real choice isn’t tag or extend. It’s extend, or pay more for the same temporary arrangement while his price keeps rising.

And that’s the thing I built two weeks ago and it keeps getting sharper. A player on a franchise tag is simultaneously the most expensive and the least secure man on a roster. Maximum current cost, zero future guarantee, and neither side has any incentive to think beyond February.

Which means Stephen Jones’s warning is now live rather than hypothetical, and the arithmetic is genuinely frightening.

Thirty-five and a half million a year for Pickens, projected, plus whatever Lamb is already on. Two receivers at the top of a market in a league with a hard salary cap.

And here’s the structural problem underneath it, which is the thing I’d want you to understand rather than the number.

Two receivers cannot both be your best weapon, because a football has one destination per play.

There were twenty-nine pass attempts against Arizona. Lamb and Pickens took fifteen of them, which is fifty-two percent. A target is a zero-sum object. Every ball thrown to one is a ball not thrown to the other.

So paying two men top-of-market money for the same finite resource is not like paying a quarterback and a left tackle, who both contribute on every snap. It’s buying two claims on one football.

Which is why so few teams do it, and why Stephen Jones said he didn’t know many who could.

And the honest counter, because there is one and it’s good. Two elite receivers do create something neither one does alone, which is a coverage problem that can’t be solved with one adjustment. A safety who leans toward Lamb has further to travel to help the corner on Pickens. That’s real, it’s worth money, and I made that exact argument from a source last week.

So the question isn’t whether two great receivers are worth having. It’s whether they’re worth thirty-five million each in a capped league where you also have a defense ranked twenty-seventh.

And that’s a roster-construction question, not a football one, and it belongs in the front office column of my ledger rather than anybody’s play sheet.
