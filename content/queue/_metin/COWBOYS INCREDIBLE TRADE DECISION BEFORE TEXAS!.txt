First, the terms, precisely.

Dallas sends a 2028 second-round pick and a conditional sixth-round pick to Pittsburgh. Dallas gets Joey Porter Junior.

Let me explain that word conditional, because people hear it and skip past it, and it matters.

A conditional pick is a pick that only changes hands, or only changes value, if something specific happens. The two teams agree on a trigger in advance. It might be a number of games played. It might be a snap percentage. It might be whether the player is still on the roster at a certain date, or whether he reaches a statistical benchmark.

Why do teams do that? Because it lets two sides who disagree about a player's value make a deal anyway. Pittsburgh thinks he'll be great. Dallas worries about the unknowns. Rather than argue over one number, you build a bridge. If he plays a lot, Pittsburgh gets more. If he doesn't, Dallas pays less.

So that sixth-round pick is not a flat cost. It's a bet neither side had to win in the room.

Now, the second-round pick, and the date on it.

This is a 2028 second. Not a 2027 second. And I told you yesterday that this specific difference was the thing I'd insist on, so let me explain properly why, because it's the most useful front-office concept in this whole video.

Draft picks lose value the further out they sit, for three reasons.

Reason one is replacement time. If you trade next year's second-round pick, you have one offseason to make up for it. If you trade a pick three years out, you have three offseasons, three trade deadlines, and three free agency periods to acquire extra capital.

Reason two is uncertainty. Nobody knows where a 2028 second-round pick will land. If Dallas is good, it's a late second, which is a lesser player. If Dallas is bad, it's an early second. You're trading a range, not a player, and ranges are psychologically cheaper than certainties.

Reason three is roster timing. By 2028, Dallas will have had two more drafts to stock its roster. The pick you're giving up is one you'd be using on a team that's already been restocked twice.

And remember what I showed you yesterday about the alternative. If Dallas had sent next year's second, that draft class becomes one pick in round one, then nothing until round four, then a pick in round six. That isn't a draft. That's three lottery tickets.

Pushing it to 2028 keeps next year's class intact. That is the single smartest detail in this entire transaction, and whoever insisted on it in that negotiation earned their money.

Now the honest counterweight, because I gave you this yesterday too and I'm not dropping it just because the trade happened.

Future picks feel free and they are not. A 2028 second-round pick is a real football player. The team that trades one always, eventually, misses it. Pushing the bill into the future is a smaller problem than paying it now. It is not no problem.

But here's the detail that genuinely changes this trade from a close call into something I like.

Joey Porter Junior is not asking Dallas for an extension.

That's being reported in multiple places, including by Calvin Watkins of the Dallas Morning News.

Read that again, because it dismantles my main objection from yesterday.

Yesterday I told you the real price isn't a second-round pick, it's a second-round pick plus a top-of-the-market contract. I said he's not getting traded and then quietly playing out a deal he's already unhappy about, so whoever trades for him is signing up for the extension too, and that you cannot skip that step.

Turns out you can skip that step. And it changes the math completely.

Let me explain why this is such a big deal, because it's the piece that scared off every other team.

The nightmare scenario for a general manager is paying twice. You pay in draft picks to acquire a player you've never coached, and then you immediately pay him at the top of the market based on somebody else's tape. You've committed enormous resources to a man before you've seen him in your building, in your scheme, in your locker room.

Nobody wants that deal. That's exactly why the price on Porter was dropping.

Without an extension demand, Dallas gets something fundamentally different. It gets to watch him first.

Now, the obvious question. Why would a player who wants to be paid at the top of the market agree to go play somewhere without a new contract?

Two reasons, and both are rational.

The first is that he wanted out. Remember why he was available. He's been in a contract dispute, he's been practicing in full health without playing, and the reporting pointed at personal reasons and a general sense that he didn't want to be there. If getting out is the goal, you don't attach conditions to the exit.

And honestly, that tells you something. A player who will leave without the contract he was fighting for is telling you the problem was never only the contract.

The second reason is that it might be good business for him. A talented cornerback who goes somewhere else, plays well, and helps a team win raises his own price. You don't negotiate from a bad situation. You negotiate from a good one. If he has the season he's capable of in Dallas, he'll be paid by somebody, and probably more than he's being offered now.

So this is one of those rare arrangements where both sides genuinely benefit from waiting. Those are the healthiest deals in sports.

Now, this will remind a lot of you of something, and the comparison is worth making carefully.

George Pickens.

Pickens came over from Pittsburgh and he wasn't demanding a contract either. Dallas essentially got a year of a very good young receiver and let him play, and the plan was always to decide later.

Porter is a version of the same idea. A year of a genuinely good player, no immediate strings, and a decision to be made down the road about whether the relationship continues.

And look at how the Pickens version is going. Ninety-three catches, fourteen hundred twenty-nine yards and a second-team All-Pro season last year. Eleven targets and a third-and-ten conversion in traffic last Sunday. That audition worked.

But I want to be careful here, because I'd be selling you something if I only told you the good half.

The Pickens model has a bill attached. Because the audition worked, Dallas now faces a franchise tag decision on him worth roughly twenty-seven million dollars, with the July fifteenth extension deadline already passed, which means no long-term deal is possible this season.

So Dallas is now running two auditions at the same time, at two positions, with two expensive decisions arriving in the same offseason.

That's the risk nobody is mentioning. Rentals feel free in October. They all come due at once in March.

I'm not saying don't do it. I'm saying the front office had better have a plan for the moment both bills land on the same desk.

Now, my scheme objection. And I'm going to drop it, on air, and tell you exactly why.

Yesterday I raised a question that almost nobody else was asking. I said Joey Porter Junior is a long, physical, press-style cornerback, which is a man-coverage profile, and that Christian Parker runs a Fangio-influenced defense that played zone on roughly seventy-eight percent of its charted coverage snaps in the opener. I asked whether Dallas would be paying premium value for a skill its scheme uses less than other schemes do.

Here's the counter-argument I've now heard, and it's better than my point was.

Christian Parker came from the Philadelphia Eagles. And last season, the Eagles ran man coverage at the highest rate in the entire National Football League. The highest in the league.

So the idea that this coordinator doesn't know what to do with a man-coverage cornerback is not a strong idea. He spent last year inside the most man-heavy defense in football. Before that he was in Denver, where he coached Pat Surtain the Second into a Defensive Player of the Year, and Surtain is about as pure a man-coverage corner as this league has.

I was wrong to treat the zone percentage from one football game as a statement of philosophy. Seventy-eight percent zone against the Giants in Week One tells you what this coach called when he had almost no cornerbacks he trusted. It doesn't tell you what he believes.

Coaches play zone when their corners can't survive alone. Give a coach corners who can, and the call sheet changes.

So I'm withdrawing the scheme concern. And the reason I'm genuinely excited about this now is the next part.

Because Dallas doesn't have one man-coverage cornerback. It has two.

DaRon Bland began his NFL career as a dominant man-coverage corner. That's how he made his name. Add Porter to that, and suddenly you have the foundation for a completely different style of defense than the one we've been watching.

And here's where it gets genuinely interesting, because this one trade might quietly solve three separate things I have complained about on this channel for a month.

Follow the dominoes with me.

Domino one. If your two outside cornerbacks can handle their receivers one-on-one, you don't need two deep safeties guarding against disaster. And what does that let you do? It lets you load the box.

Let me explain what loading the box actually means and why it matters. The box is the area near the line of scrimmage. Every extra defender you put in there is another body against the run and another potential rusher. The only reason you can't do it all the time is that every man you move forward is a man not covering somebody deep.

Man coverage outside buys you those bodies. That's the whole trade.

Domino two. Caleb Downs.

We have now spent four separate videos arguing about where this young man should play. I argued for nickel in August because that's where his instincts and physicality show up most. Then P.J. Locke tore his plantar fascia and I changed to using him at free safety in situations. Then Jalen Thompson went out for a month and I said it wasn't a debate anymore, it was arithmetic, and he had to go play deep.

And I said that reluctantly, because moving him deep takes him away from the thing he's best at.

Now look what this trade does. If Dallas can play more man coverage outside, it needs fewer deep safeties. Which means Caleb Downs can play closer to the line of scrimmage, where he just stuffed Derrick Henry on fourth and one and stopped a quarterback in the open field.

A cornerback trade may have just solved my safety problem. Not by signing a safety. By making the deep safety less necessary.

That's the kind of thing only roster construction can do, and it's beautiful when it works.

Domino three. Quinnen Williams.

If you're loading the box and playing man behind it, your defensive line's job changes. You're no longer asking interior linemen to hold ground and read while the back end plays soft. You're asking them to attack.

And attacking is the entire point of Quinnen Williams. I made a whole video about this. He built his reputation as a penetrating three-technique, a one-gap player who shoots a gap and wrecks the play before it develops, and Dallas has been asking him to two-gap, absorb double teams and read. I called it a Ferrari being driven like a minivan. Adam Archuleta on CBS said almost exactly the same thing.

More man coverage and a loaded box is the context in which Quinnen Williams gets to be Quinnen Williams.

So add it up. One cornerback, and the three things I've been complaining about since September, the Downs alignment, the Williams misuse, and a scheme that can't afford to be aggressive, all get a path forward.

That is why best player available can be smarter than shopping for a need. You don't just add a player. You add options.

Now the honest counterweight, because I promised you the complete picture.

Man coverage with a thin safety room is high-variance football.

Here's what that means in practice. In zone, when something goes slightly wrong, you usually give up eight yards. In man, when something goes wrong and there's nobody behind it, you give up a touchdown.

So Dallas would be trading a slow bleed for a boom-or-bust style. Fewer easy completions, more explosive plays against. If Porter and Bland win their matchups, this defense looks transformed. If either one loses, with Hooker, Locke and Thompson all unavailable, there is nothing behind them.

That's a real risk and anybody telling you otherwise is selling.

Now let me address the thing I think is most impressive about this, which isn't the player at all. It's the timing.

For weeks on this channel we've been saying Dallas needs an edge rusher and a defensive back, and that it should go get them.

Here's the problem with that, and it's the honest reason nothing had happened. In the first month of an NFL season, nobody is selling.

Think about it from the other side. It's early October. Every team still believes it has a season. Nobody is giving away their best linebacker or their best pass rusher in week four, because nobody has admitted anything yet. And whoever is available this early is usually available for a reason, which means they don't move the needle.

Let me give you the mechanism, because it explains the entire trade market.

Early in the season, supply is almost zero and prices are high, because the only sellers are teams that have already given up and there aren't any yet. As you get closer to the deadline, supply rises and prices fall, because more teams have quietly accepted their situation.

So the one category of genuinely good player who is acquirable in September is the unhappy one. A player whose own situation has broken down, whose team's leverage has collapsed because everyone knows he wants out.

That was Joey Porter Junior. He wasn't the best player Dallas could have wanted. He was the best player who was actually purchasable on the thirtieth of September.

And that's what going all in looks like in October. It isn't elegant. It's a little late, and you'd rather have done it in March when there were more options on the table. But it's real, and I'll take a front office that acts in October over one that explains itself in January.

I also want to point out what this trade quietly admits, because it's the most honest thing this organization has done all month.

Back in August, we said on this channel that the goal was never a historically great defense. The goal was good enough. Something around twentieth in the league, so that Dak Prescott and this offense could win games.

Dallas is thirtieth in expected points added per play, thirty-first in total expected points added, and dead last in success rate.

This trade is the front office looking at that and saying out loud that it was too optimistic in August. That's not an embarrassment. That's a correction, and correcting in week four is infinitely better than correcting in December.

And the broadest point. One cornerback, however good, cannot repair a defense that ranks thirtieth. This defense is a list of problems, and Dallas just crossed off the biggest one on the list. Crossing off one item is not finishing the list.

So my line from yesterday stands exactly as I said it. If Dallas trades, it can't be just one. This team needs a defensive back and an edge rusher.
