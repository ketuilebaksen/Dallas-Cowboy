Now, here’s the checklist, because this video handles that material the way I wish everything did. Eight things, and I want you to have them, because they’re the positive version of everything I’ve been complaining about for three weeks.

One. It states what the claim is not, immediately. In the first forty seconds: it is not a confirmed plan and nobody should treat it as a done deal.

Two. It identifies the load-bearing word. The key word though is conversation. That is a narrator telling you exactly where the uncertainty lives, which is the single most helpful sentence a reporter can write.

Three. It states the absence of evidence explicitly. Aikman has not been announced as a candidate for a specific position, and there is no indication the Cowboys are preparing to hand him control of football operations.

Four. It names its source. Mike Florio, on Pro Football Talk. A person and a program, both of which can be checked.

Five. It gives the source’s reasoning, not just the source’s conclusion. Florio doesn’t just say it won’t happen; he says why, which is that Jerry Jones has held the general manager title for decades and isn’t surrendering it. Reasoning is what lets you disagree, and a claim you can’t disagree with isn’t an argument.

Six. It reframes the question to the one that actually matters. The biggest issue would not simply be whether Aikman wants to work in a front office. It would be how much authority the Cowboys would actually be willing to give him.

Seven. It separates title from responsibility. A title can sound impressive, but the day-to-day responsibilities and influence behind it are what really count.

Tell number twenty-four. Prefer the perishable.

When two sources conflict, the one containing the most perishable detail is almost always the more current one.

Here’s what I mean by perishable. Some statements can only have been written inside a very narrow window of time, because the thing they describe changes constantly. Other statements stay true for weeks or forever, which means they tell you nothing about when they were written.

A Thursday practice participation level is perishable to the day. Full, limited, did not participate. That designation exists for about seventy-two hours and then it’s replaced. Somebody had to look at a specific piece of paper on a specific afternoon to write it down. You cannot produce that sentence from a blend of everything ever written about a football team, because it has never been written before.

A season record is durable. Two and four stays two and four until the next game, which could be a week or two weeks.

And a phrase like entered its bye week at two and four is durable in a nastier way, because it stays true forever after the fact. If Dallas had a bye at some point this season while sitting at two and four, that sentence remains accurate for the rest of the year and for the rest of time, which means it is completely useless for telling you what day it is.

So the test is: which claim required somebody to look at something recent?

And the answer is clearly the injury report. Specific body parts, specific names, specific participation levels, two teams, and a game-day quarterback situation that the other video doesn’t mention at all.

So my lean, stated as a lean and not a conclusion, is that the Philadelphia game is real, and that today’s bye-week line is either stale or wrong. But I want to be honest that there’s a version where I’m the one who’s confused, which is that Dallas’s bye already happened earlier in the season at two and four, and this video is describing something true about three weeks ago as if it were current. That would make the sentence accurate and misleading at the same time, which is a combination I’ve run into about six times this month.

Now, I want to make the general version of tell twenty-four explicit, because it ties the whole framework together and I think it’s the deepest thing on the list.

Perishable content is expensive. Durable content is free.

To write a practice report you need access, or at minimum you need to go find one and read it. It costs somebody something. And its reward is that it dates itself honestly, so you can tell exactly when it was made.

To write that the Cowboys are one of the most prominent franchises in professional sports, that expectations are enormous every season, that a bye week can offer a team a chance to regroup, and that fans want to know whether the coaching staff can correct the problems, you need nothing at all. Those sentences are free, they are all true, and they have no date.

Which is why the stuff I keep calling filler is not an accident of laziness. It’s the cheapest available product, and it never expires, which means it can be published any day of any season about any team.

And that’s the same engine as everything else on this list. Two weeks ago I gave you the unifying theory, which was that every tell measures unfalsifiability. Content that cannot be wrong never has to be good.

Tell twenty-four is that theory applied to time. Content that cannot go stale never has to be current.

So when you’re deciding which of two sources to believe, don’t ask which one sounds more confident. Ask which one would have been impossible to write last month.

Now, one more thing about sourcing in this video, and it’s a genuine problem I can’t solve.

The video attributes its reporting to Mike Florio of Pro Football Talk. And then later, when it gets to Aikman’s own comments, it says in comments to Mike Fiser.

I cannot tell whether those are two people or one person’s name mangled twice.

And it matters enormously, because of this: one source quoted twice is not two sources.

If Florio and the second name are the same man, then this entire story rests on one reporter’s assessment, repeated, and the second mention is not corroboration. It just looks like corroboration, because the name appeared again.

If they’re two different people, then you have a reporter’s analysis plus a separate reporter who actually spoke to Aikman, which is a meaningfully stronger foundation.

That’s circular sourcing, and it’s one of the most common ways a weak story gets strong-looking. A claim gets repeated across outlets, each one citing the last, and by the fourth repetition it feels corroborated when nothing has been added since the first.

The test is the same one I’d use anywhere. Trace it back. How many people actually know this, as opposed to how many places have printed it?

Alright. Now the football, or in this case the front-office analysis, because Florio’s point is correct and I want to give you the sharper version of it.

He says the real issue isn’t whether Aikman wants the job. It’s how much authority Dallas would give him.

That’s exactly right, and let me tell you why the title question is almost worthless.

Assistant general manager is a job title with no fixed meaning anywhere in football. In one building, the assistant general manager runs the draft. In another, he handles the salary cap. In another, he’s a succession plan being groomed in public. In another, he answers the phone.

So if Dallas announced tomorrow that Troy Aikman was the assistant general manager, you would have learned almost nothing, because the title doesn’t specify the work.

Here’s what would actually tell you. Three questions, and they’re the only three that matter in any front office in any sport.

Question one. Who has final say on the fifty-three man roster?

Not who has input. Not who’s in the room. Who, when there is a disagreement about whether a player is on this team, makes the call that ends the conversation.

Question two. Who has final say on hiring and firing the head coach?

That’s the biggest single decision a football organization makes, it gets made maybe once every four years, and the person who holds it holds the organization.

Question three. Who controls contract structure?

This is the one nobody asks about and it’s enormous. I’ve spent weeks on this channel explaining signing bonus proration and dead money. The way you structure a deal determines how much flexibility you have three years from now, which means the person who decides whether to push money into the future is deciding what the roster looks like in 2029.

Answer those three and you know exactly how much power anybody has. Everything else is advisory.

Now the honest counterweight, because I don’t want to leave you thinking advisory means worthless.

A respected voice in a room can change decisions without holding any authority at all. Sometimes the single most valuable function in an organization is the ability to tell an owner that something is a bad idea and be taken seriously. Very few people can do that, and the ones who can are worth more than their titles suggest.

But that value depends entirely on whether the owner actually listens, and that is completely unobservable from outside the building. You will never be able to check it. Which makes it, by my own standards, exactly the kind of claim I should be careful about, in either direction. I can’t tell you an advisory role would be hollow and I can’t tell you it would be meaningful, because the thing that decides it happens in private.

Now, the part of this video I find most interesting, and it raises a question that applies far beyond Dallas.

The source says there’s a difference between understanding the game as a Hall of Fame quarterback and managing an NFL organization. And that evaluating talent, building a roster, and handling the day-to-day work of a front office require a different set of responsibilities.

That’s correct, and let me go further than the video does, because I think the gap is bigger and more specific than people realize.

Playing quarterback and scouting quarterbacks are almost opposite cognitive tasks.

Playing is recognition under extreme time pressure. You have between two and three seconds, you’re reading a shape that’s actively lying to you, and you’re executing a physical skill while doing it. Feedback is immediate and brutal. You find out whether you were right about a quarter of a second later.

Scouting is slow probabilistic projection. You’re looking at a twenty-one-year-old and estimating what he becomes at twenty-five, under coaching that doesn’t exist yet, on a roster that hasn’t been built. Feedback arrives in three or four years, by which time a dozen other variables have contaminated the result, and you will frequently never know whether you were right.

Nothing about being excellent at the first prepares you for the second. They share a vocabulary and almost nothing else.

And then there’s the problem that I think is the real one, and it’s a little uncomfortable.

A great player’s biggest handicap in evaluation is that he was an outlier.

His intuitions about what the job requires were formed by being exceptional at it. Which is the worst possible sample size in the worst possible position on the distribution. He knows, with total certainty, what he needed in order to succeed. He has no direct knowledge at all of what the median prospect needs, because he was never the median prospect.

And I made almost exactly this argument last week about something else, which is a nice confirmation that the principle generalizes. When I was explaining why a Heisman Trophy finalist ends up on a practice squad, I said that in college a great team has a talent advantage, and a quarterback operating behind a talent advantage gets cleaner pockets and more margin, and that in the National Football League nobody has a talent advantage. The skill didn’t transfer because the conditions changed.

Same shape here. The expertise was built under conditions that don’t apply to the new job.

Now the genuine both-sides, because this is not a disqualification and the source says so too.

Plenty of former players have become excellent executives. And there are specific, real advantages that only a former player brings.

The first is credibility in a locker room, which is not sentimental. A player who believes the man upstairs understands what he’s being asked to do behaves differently than one who doesn’t.

The second is more concrete and I think it’s the strongest argument. A former quarterback can evaluate whether a scheme is coachable. Not whether it’s clever on a whiteboard, but whether a twenty-three-year-old can execute it on third and seven with ninety thousand people screaming. That is a genuinely rare and valuable judgment and almost nobody in a front office is qualified to make it.

So the honest verdict is that it’s a different skill set which needs to be tested rather than assumed. Which is the same thing you’d say about anybody moving from any job to any other job, and the only reason it needs saying is that a famous name makes people skip the step.
