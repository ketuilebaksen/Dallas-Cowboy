Now, the part I owe you most, which is where I was wrong.

I spent four days building a thesis about this football game, and it went like this.

Philadelphia’s defense is coached by Vic Fangio. A Fangio defense plays two safeties deep, keeps the box light, rushes four, doesn’t blitz much, and operates on bend-but-don’t-break. And the central deal that design offers you is: take the run, take four yards, take the clock, and I will make sure nothing goes seventy yards over my head.

And then I said, and I’ll quote myself because I was pleased with it at the time: the one thing a Fangio defense gives you is the one thing this offense cannot do. Dallas is thirty-first in rushing. It cannot take the run.

I called it the tightest matchup argument I’d been able to build all season.

Dallas ran the ball productively and won the game.

So let me be precise about what was right and what was wrong, because the split matters.

The mechanism was right. Fangio offered the run. Everything I said about what that design does and why it does it holds up, and the video describes exactly the game I predicted the shape of. Dallas established the run, Philadelphia had to deal with the ground game while still accounting for receivers, and the offense didn’t have to ask the quarterback to rescue every possession.

That’s my thesis, running forward, happening.

The conclusion was wrong. I said Dallas couldn’t take what was being offered. Dallas took it.

And here is the specific thing I missed, and when I tell you what it is you’ll see why it stings.

Dallas used Broderick Jones as an extra offensive lineman.

A sixth lineman. An additional big body on the field, giving the defense a different blocking surface to handle.

Now watch what that does to my own argument.

A light box is not a philosophy. It’s a bet on arithmetic. Fangio keeps fewer defenders near the line of scrimmage because he’s wagering that your blockers can’t convert the numbers advantage into enough yards to beat him.

Put a sixth offensive lineman on the field and you have changed the arithmetic of that bet. Not the talent. The count.

And I should have seen this coming, because I gave you the tool on Tuesday and then failed to apply it to my own prediction forty-eight hours later.

On Tuesday I explained the difference between zone and gap running. Zone asks five men to each win an individual battle while moving laterally. Gap scheme pulls a blocker across the formation and buys you a numbers advantage at one point. And I said, in those words: zone requires winning, and gap requires arithmetic. If your line can’t consistently win laterally, you stop asking five men to each beat a man and you start buying a two-on-one at one location.

An extra offensive lineman is that same principle, applied with personnel instead of with a pulling guard. It’s arithmetic rather than victory.

So I had the correct framework in hand on Tuesday, used it to argue that Dallas should run counter and power, and then on Thursday and Friday told you Dallas couldn’t run against a light box. Those two positions don’t fit together and I didn’t notice.

That’s the error, and it’s not a data error. I had all the facts. It’s a failure to carry my own reasoning across three days, which is a less forgivable kind of mistake than being handed bad numbers.

Now, the thing that was vindicated, and I think it’s the sharpest distinction I made all month, so I’m going to claim it.

Now, the offensive line detail that I think is genuinely insightful, and it’s the kind of thing I’d never have thought to say.

The video says of Tyler Guyton that he wasn’t being asked to spend the entire night retreating and protecting the pocket. He could attack, get into defenders, and help open lanes. And it calls that a different kind of evening for an offensive tackle.

That’s a real observation and let me extend it.

A tackle who gets to attack is a different player from a tackle who only retreats.

Pass protection is reactive. You’re moving backward, your base is compromised, you’re absorbing force rather than delivering it, and you lose by any single mistake over two and a half seconds. It is the hardest thing offensive linemen do and it has almost nothing in common with the other half of the job.

Run blocking is proactive. You go forward, you initiate contact, you’re the one delivering force, and you win by displacement.

Those are nearly opposite athletic tasks, and an offense that only ever passes has put every one of its tackles in the harder of the two, all night, every night.

So a run-heavy plan does not only help the running back. It changes what you’re asking five men to do with their bodies, and it lets a tackle who’s good at one half of the job spend some of the evening doing that half.

And it has a compounding effect, because the pass protection gets better too. A defensive end who has to honor a run cannot take his widest, deepest, most aggressive rush angle off the snap. I priced that last week at a tenth or two of a second, which is maybe eight percent of a pocket.

Which means the thing the video reports — a productive running game and protection for Dak Prescott, in the same sentence — isn’t two achievements. It’s one achievement and its consequence.

Now, the most important sentence in this video, and it’s methodological.

The source criticises two specific play calls. And then he says this: that’s a fair debate even after a win. You can appreciate the overall game plan and still dislike an individual decision. For me, the standard shouldn’t change just because the final score makes everything feel better afterward.

The standard shouldn’t change because of the final score.

I want to make that as sharp as I can, because this is the week it matters and it’s the hardest discipline in all of sports analysis.

A win is the most dangerous moment for honest evaluation.

Here’s why. After a loss, every decision gets examined, and most of them get examined too harshly. After a win, every decision gets retroactively validated, and nobody examines anything, because the scoreboard appears to have answered all the questions.

But a play call doesn’t know the final score. The decision was made with the information available at that moment, and its quality was fixed at that moment, and nothing that happened in the fourth quarter reaches backward to improve it.

And notice this is the exact companion to a principle I gave you last week. I called it asymmetric luck accounting: if you’re going to discount wins for luck, you have to discount losses for luck too, and almost nobody does, because the appeal of the method is that it only points one direction.

Today’s version is the same shape pointed at decisions instead of results. If you’d have criticised a call after a loss, you have to criticise it after a win, and almost nobody does.

So let me actually do it, on the two calls this source raises, because that’s the honest exercise.

Call one. Dallas is backed up near its own goal line. After an incomplete pass to Pickens, they hand the ball to Javonte Williams on third and eight, and then punt.

Now, I want to steelman that harder than the video does, because there’s a real argument for it that’s about more than field position.

A third-and-eight run from your own end zone is a punt with extra steps, and sometimes a punt with extra steps is the correct play. The downside scenarios from inside your own ten are catastrophic and asymmetric. A sack is a safety. A strip is a defensive touchdown. An interception is points. The distribution of outcomes on a pass from there has a fat tail on the wrong side, and a handoff truncates that tail at the cost of a conversion you probably weren’t getting anyway.

Third and eight converts somewhere around a third of the time at best. So you’re declining a one-in-three chance to eliminate a small probability of handing over seven points. Depending on the score and the time, that can absolutely be right.

And the video’s criticism is sharper than it first appears, because it doesn’t say the call was wrong. It says Dallas accepted a conservative way out of a difficult spot, and that’s different from saying the offense had no other option.

That’s the correct version of the complaint. Not that the decision was indefensible, but that it was a decision, and it should be named as one rather than treated as the only thing available.

Call two is the one I’d actually argue with, and the video does too.

Dallas is near Philadelphia’s goal line. Third and eight. They hand the ball to Hunter Luepke, a fullback. He gains three. They kick a field goal.

That one’s harder to defend, and here’s why the arithmetic is different.

Near the opponent’s goal line, the catastrophic outcomes mostly disappear. A sack is not a safety. An incompletion costs you nothing but the down. The interception risk is real but the geometry is cramped and the field is short.

So you’ve eliminated almost none of the downside, because the downside was already small, and you’ve paid for it with a conversion attempt you declined to make.

And the difference between a touchdown and a field goal is four points, which in a one-score game is the whole game.

The video puts it exactly right: I get the appeal of protecting those points, but what I question is how much chance that call gave you to extend the drive.

So, for the record, and after a win: I think the first call is defensible and the second one isn’t, and I’d have said the same thing about both if Dallas had lost by three.

Now the operational detail, which is the kind of thing that only somebody watching closely ever catches.

Before halftime, Dallas moved the ball without immediately using a timeout, and CeeDee Lamb helped get the offense into scoring position. And the video reports a twenty-five second gap between snaps on consecutive completions.

Twenty-five seconds. In a two-minute situation.

Let me explain why that number is bad, because it sounds small.

In a two-minute drill, the play clock is your opponent. The reason you push tempo is that every second between snaps is a second subtracted from the number of plays you’ll get, and the number of plays is the only thing that determines whether you reach the end zone or settle for three.

Twenty-five seconds between snaps on a completion means the offense was not operating at tempo. And it compounds, because two of those is fifty seconds, which at the end of a half is an entire possession’s worth of time, converted into nothing.

And the video’s framing is right: getting points was good, whether Dallas gave itself the best chance at a touchdown is a separate question.

Those are two different questions and the scoreboard answers the first one and tells you nothing about the second.

Now, the piece of news in here that I think matters most for the rest of the season, and nobody is going to lead with it.

Shemar James took the green dot.

Let me explain what that is, because I covered it weeks ago and it deserves a second pass.

One defensive player on the field wears a helmet with a green dot on the back. That dot means he has the radio receiver, which means the coordinator’s voice comes to him, and he relays the call to the other ten men. He’s the one who hears the adjustment, repeats it, and makes sure everybody is lined up before the snap.

It is the single most important non-physical job on a defense, and it has nothing to do with how fast anybody runs.

And the video says James handled that responsibility in place of Jaishawn Barham, and that Schottenheimer praised his command.

Two things about that.

The first is that Barham is a rookie, which I learned on Tuesday. Putting the radio on a rookie is a vote of confidence and also a heavy load, because a man who is still learning his own assignment is now also responsible for everybody else’s.

The second is that this is a direct intervention on exactly the problem I’ve been describing for three weeks.

Everything I’ve said about this defense has come back to communication. Missed assignments. Two defenders who think they have the same receiver. A young defender thinking too much. And the line I keep repeating: you cannot trade for assignment clarity, because the market sells players and it does not sell communication.

Moving the green dot is one of the very few things a team can do about that in October. It costs nothing, it requires no draft picks, and it changes who is responsible for organizing ten men before every snap.

And the video says the exact right thing about what to watch next, which is that for James the job goes beyond getting himself to the football — he has to help teammates get organized when the offense changes its look.

That’s the test. Not tackles. Whether the defense is lined up correctly when an opponent shows them something unexpected.

And it bears on a falsifiable condition I set last week. I said that if communication errors and blown assignments go down over three weeks, the Pettine arrangement worked and I was right to walk back my two-cooks concern.

I now have to add a confound to my own test, and I’d rather name it than quietly benefit from it. If the communication improves, I will not be able to tell whether that was Pettine, the install settling, or a new man wearing the green dot. Three candidate explanations, one observation, and the condition I wrote can’t distinguish them.

That’s a badly designed test and it’s mine. The best I can do is report all three and refuse to credit any one of them.
