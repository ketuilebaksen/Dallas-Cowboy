DeMarvion Overshown says he has been medically cleared and will return Monday night against Philadelphia, after missing the last five games with a hamstring injury.

The injury happens in the involuntary moment, the change of direction you didn’t plan, the sudden acceleration from a dead stop when a back breaks the wrong way.

Which is why a player can look perfect in individual drills and still not be available, and why the practice report distinction between individual work and team work matters more than the words full or limited.

Now let me put the two injuries together, because this produces the one genuinely new idea I have today, and I think it’s a good one.

The source makes a smart argument. Before the Packers game, you could at least understand listening to an offer on Overshown. After losing a starting linebacker to an Achilles, you can’t. Draft picks are useful, but so are actual players when you have to line up against Philadelphia in a few days.

That’s correct, and it’s my own replace-him test applied properly. Who takes the snaps, at what price, and what happens to the group.

But I want to add a layer, because there’s something underneath it that nobody ever says.

Call it phantom surplus.

Here’s the idea. When a starter is injured, the healthy players behind him start looking like a crowd. Somebody steps up, somebody else covers that man’s old role, and suddenly the position group looks deeper than it is. And the player on injured reserve, who isn’t playing and isn’t visibly needed, starts to look like a tradeable asset.

But that depth was never real. It was a measurement taken during an injury. Every snap being covered by a backup is a snap not being covered by whoever that backup was supposed to be behind, all the way down the chart. You’re not deep. You’re borrowing from yourself, and the ledger only balances while everybody else stays upright.

Which is why Dallas went from a plausible Overshown seller to an urgent linebacker buyer in four days, in the same position group, with nothing changing except one Achilles.

So here’s the rule. Surplus is only surplus when everybody is healthy, and nobody is ever healthy. Which means a depth chart measured in the middle of an injury always overstates your depth, and a player who looks expendable while he’s hurt is the most likely man on your roster to be essential the moment he isn’t.

And there’s a market consequence that makes it worse, which I’ve touched on before in a different context. Your trade value and your need move in opposite directions at exactly the wrong times. The moment you’re comfortable enough to sell, buyers know you don’t have to, so the price is low. The moment you need to buy, sellers know you have to, so the price is high. You are never trading into a neutral market, and the direction it tilts is always away from you.

So the right answer on Overshown is the boring one, and the source gets there too. You don’t move him now. You can revisit it closer to the November tenth deadline, when you know whether the Achilles is three weeks or the season, and when you know whether this team is two and five or four and four.

And keeping him costs nothing except patience, which is the cheapest thing in football and the hardest to buy.
