Let’s get into it.

Here’s what happened. On Saturday the seventh of November, Dallas elevated wide receiver Jordan Hudson and tight end James Mitchell from the practice squad for the game in Indianapolis. Mike Garafolo of NFL Network reported the moves and Todd Archer of ESPN tied them to the injury questions around George Pickens and Jake Ferguson.

And on the same afternoon, Dallas placed rookie first-round pick Malachi Lawrence on injured reserve with an ankle injury.

Now, before anything else, let me explain why two practice squad elevations are worth eight minutes, because this is the kind of transaction everybody scrolls past and it is the most honest signal a football team ever sends.

A press conference is a performance. An injury report is a document filed under rules, with language chosen by lawyers. A roster move costs something and is therefore a decision.

So the principle, and it’s the one I’ve been circling for a fortnight.

Watch what a team spends, not what it says.

And an elevation costs something specific, which is where the mechanics matter.

A player on the practice squad can be elevated to the game day roster three times in a season. That’s it. Three. After the third one, if you want him again, you have to sign him to the active fifty-three, which means somebody else comes off it.

So every elevation is one of three bullets, and a team that fires one has told you it is worried about something.

Jordan Hudson has used exactly one of his three. Plenty left. James Mitchell has already been through at least two, because he was elevated last Saturday for Arizona as well.

Which means Mitchell is one elevation from a real roster decision. If Dallas wants him in Indianapolis and then wants him again the week after, somebody has to be cut.

That’s a live deadline nobody is talking about, and it lands the same week as the trade deadline.

Now the observation in this video that is the best piece of reasoning anybody has sent me in days, and I want to give it full credit because it’s the kind of thing you only find by looking at last week as well as this week.

KaVontae Turpin has been ruled out with a rib injury. That’s his third consecutive game out.

So the easy explanation for elevating a receiver is Turpin.

Except Dallas did not elevate a receiver in either of the previous two weeks without Turpin. They made rookie Camden Brown active as the fifth wide receiver and moved on.

Same missing player. Different response.

Which means the thing that changed between last Sunday and this Saturday is not Turpin, because Turpin was already gone.

That is a beautiful piece of work and it’s the single most useful analytical move available to anybody covering a team.

When a team responds differently to the same situation, the situation isn’t what changed.

And it’s the same shape as the arithmetic I’ve been doing all month. When two numbers don’t match, the gap is the information. Here, when two weeks with identical circumstances produce different behaviour, the difference is the information, and the difference is not the man who was already absent.

So what changed? George Pickens’s hamstring.

Let me give you the full history, because this video assembles it properly and I’d only had fragments.

Pickens is listed as questionable. He was limited in practice on all three days this week. The hamstring first appeared on the injury report on the twenty-eighth of October, and that came directly behind a shoulder issue that had already put him on the report the week before. And reporters covering the team say he has been limited in practice for about a month.

A month.

Which closes something I got wrong four days ago. I attributed his reduced snap count to a shoulder, because a shoulder was what the report said when I looked. The honest picture is a shoulder, then a hamstring, then four weeks of limited practice, which is why the coach used the plural when he said injuries.

And the hamstring is the one that matters, for the reason I’ve given you a dozen times. Hamstrings fail on reaction rather than on running. A receiver’s entire job is acceleration and the sudden change of direction that breaks a route off. That is precisely the movement that tears hamstrings, performed forty times an afternoon against a man paid to make him do it twice.

So a receiver on a month-old hamstring is not a slightly reduced receiver.

Now the fair counterpoint, which this video supplies and which I’d have supplied myself.

This is the exact path Pickens took last week. Limited all week, questionable on Friday, and then he played against Arizona. So a questionable tag by itself proves nothing. Plenty of men walk that road and suit up.

What’s different is the pattern rather than the tag. One week of limited practice is management. Four weeks of limited practice with two injuries stacked on each other is a condition.

And then the number. Against Arizona, with that hamstring, he finished with two catches for forty-eight yards on four targets.

I did the arithmetic on that two days ago and I’ll repeat the honest version. Thirty-seven of those forty-eight came on one play. So it was one explosive catch and one ordinary one, and twelve yards per target is not a rate, it’s a distribution.

But two catches for a receiver on a twenty-seven million dollar franchise tag is the kind of number a building notices, and this video’s line on it is exactly right. I can’t tell you the hamstring is the reason the ball didn’t find him more often. Nobody outside that locker room can.

That’s the correct amount of certainty and almost nobody manages it.

Now Brian Schottenheimer’s explanation, because he said something genuinely useful and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than mined for drama.

He described a limited player as part of a puzzle, and said the way Dallas protects itself is by carrying extra depth at certain spots.

That is the entire logic of both moves in one sentence, and the video’s expansion of it is the best framing of an injury report I’ve heard.

A player who might be limited isn’t a yes or a no. He’s a question. And depth is how you answer a question you can’t settle before kickoff.

Which connects directly to the four gates I built weeks ago. Is he closer, is he cleared, is he available, is he effective. Pickens is almost certainly past gate three and the entire uncertainty lives at gate four, which is the gate no report measures and the only one that affects a football game.

And then there’s the word. Schottenheimer said he’s hopeful.

Hopeful is not the same as good to go, and the video is right that everybody who heard him say it knew that. Hopeful is a feeling about a future state. Available is a status. One of those appears on a Sunday inactive list and the other doesn’t.

Now the three scenarios, because this is where the elevations earn their place.

Scenario one. Pickens plays at full speed. Hudson is a name on a game day list and you never notice him.

Scenario two, and this is the one the video calls sneaky and it’s correct. Pickens plays but can’t fully open up. Because a limited receiver is still on the field and still being accounted for by a defense, which means he occupies a coverage he can’t actually beat. And hamstrings are famous for the specific cruelty of feeling fine on the first drive and tightening in the second quarter, at which point you are down a receiver in the middle of a game with no time to plan for it.

That’s the scenario an elevation exists for. Not a star. A man who has been in the building since before week one and does not need a map.

Scenario three. He sits, and the whole passing game changes shape, because then CeeDee Lamb is the one man every coverage in Indianapolis is built around.

And here’s the problem that makes today’s second move necessary, and it’s the part that connects the two.

Normally, when a receiver is out, you lean harder on your tight end.

Jake Ferguson has an ankle injury he picked up in the first quarter against Arizona. He missed practice Wednesday and Thursday, was limited on Friday, and he’s questionable.

Luke Schoonmaker has a groin injury, and he’s questionable too.

So Dallas has a star receiver with a hamstring, a starting tight end with an ankle and a second tight end with a groin, all three carrying the same tag on the same day.

That’s a lot of Dak Prescott’s targets sitting on one list.

And the video makes a point about coaching workload that I had genuinely not considered, so let me pass it on.

You cannot script around three question marks. You have to build a game plan that works if all three play, works if two play, and works if none of them do.

That’s three versions of the same offense, prepared in one week, by a staff that has a finite number of hours.

Which reframes something I’ve spent days criticising. I have been hard on this offense for being predictable against Arizona, and I corrected myself yesterday when I learned Ferguson was lost on the opening possession. Add this, and you get a fuller picture. A staff spending its week building three offenses is a staff that has fewer hours to make any one of them clever.
