So today I want to give you the constructive half of something I showed you yesterday, because this headline accidentally demonstrates the technique.

Yesterday I gave you tell twenty-five, which is that title fragments recur. Titles get built out of reusable modules. A name, an intensifier, a transaction-shaped noun, a suffix for false recency.

Here’s the tool that falls out of it, and I think it’s the most practical thing on the whole list.

Strip the prefix and see what’s left.

Because if you delete the bolted-on module and the remaining sentence is still true, then somebody attached a lie to the front of honest work, and the work underneath might be worth your time. And if you delete the module and what’s left is also false, it’s fabricated all the way down.

Today the prefix lies and the main clause tells the truth, four words apart, in the same headline. Could add is a hedge. Blockbuster trade is an assertion. Two completely different epistemic registers, bolted together, and the honest one is the part nobody reads.

Now the football argument, and the video makes the right one so let me extend it.

It says Kenny Clark, Quinnen Williams and a third interior lineman give Dallas a solid interior defensive line, and asks you to imagine an edge rusher working outside while those three clog the middle.

That’s correct, and here’s the geometry that makes it correct.

Interior pressure and edge pressure do opposite things to a quarterback, and that’s why you need both.

Interior pressure collapses the pocket forward. It takes away the quarterback’s platform, shortens his throwing space, and removes the option of stepping up. Edge pressure comes from behind and outside, and it forces him to step up into the pocket to escape it.

Now notice the trap. If you only have edge pressure, the quarterback steps up into clean space and throws. If you only have interior pressure, he slides and escapes to the edge. Each one has an escape hatch, and the escape hatch is the other one.

Which means the two kinds of pressure are not additive. They are multiplicative. And a team with a genuinely good interior and no edge is not halfway to a pass rush. It’s close to nowhere, which is exactly what eight sacks in six games looks like.

That’s the case for Sweat, and it’s a better case than the one about his sack total, because it’s about what his presence does to three other players.

And the video adds a detail I like, which is the schedule twist. Arizona comes to Dallas the week after Philadelphia.
