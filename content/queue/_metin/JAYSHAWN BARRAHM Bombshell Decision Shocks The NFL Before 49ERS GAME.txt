First, the name, because this is the longest-running unresolved item in my notes and the way it resolved is instructive.

Thirteen days ago I was handed a transcript naming a Dallas linebacker and I wrote him down as Justin Baron, flagged him, and refused to guess. Nine days later a different video gave me Jon Barum, and I said out loud that I wondered whether Baron and Barum were the same man mangled two different ways by two different machines, and asked whether Dallas had one rookie linebacker in that role or two.

Four more spellings, which makes six across three weeks, and today’s title offers a seventh. Jayshawn Barraham.

So I’m going to say what I can say and label what I can’t.

He is a rookie linebacker. A third-round pick. He held the green dot after DeMarvion Overshown’s early-season injury. He lost it to Shemar James before the Philadelphia game. And his snap counts have gone twenty-one, sixteen, three.

That is one man, not two, and the hypothesis I raised nine days ago was correct. Baron and Barum are the same person.

What I still cannot do is spell him with confidence, and three weeks of not guessing has produced six corruptions pointing at a name I can’t triangulate. So he stays on the list, with a complete profile and no confirmed spelling, which is the most frustrating entry I’ve ever had.

And there’s a lesson in that which I’d rather state than hide.

Collating corruptions works when the errors fail in different directions. It fails when they all fail the same way.

I’ve resolved four names this month by that method. Caleb Downs from Caleb D S plus Downs. Gardner Minshew from three bad versions across two sources. P. J. Locke from Lockach and Lock in one video. Ed Werder from Edwarder and Word.

Every one of those worked because one machine dropped the front of the name and another dropped the back, and between them they preserved the whole thing.

This one doesn’t work, because all six corruptions have mangled the same syllable in the same place. Baron, Barum, Baram, Barm, Barraham. They agree on the beginning and disagree on everything after it, which means the overlap contains no new information.

So the method has a limit and today is the limit.

Now the football, because the snap count story is a genuinely good piece of work and it needs careful handling.

Twenty-one snaps against Philadelphia. Sixteen against Arizona. Three against Indianapolis.

Three snaps is roughly five percent of a defensive afternoon.

Now, before anybody draws a conclusion, let me do the thing I do with every number, which is ask what else could produce it.

Three snaps could mean a coaching staff has lost faith in a player. It could mean a game plan that happened not to use his package. It could mean an opponent’s personnel groupings never created the situation he’s in for. It could mean a minor injury nobody reported. Or it could mean the two men ahead of him simply played well and never came off.

Five explanations, one number.

And the video does something I want to credit immediately, because it’s rare and it’s exactly right.

A snap count is a useful piece of evidence, but it cannot explain everything behind the rotation. It tells us who played and roughly how often, not all the details of what the staff saw in practice, which personnel groupings it preferred, or how the opponent affected its decisions.

That’s correct, and it’s the same discipline I asked for a fortnight ago when everybody was reading George Pickens’s snap count as a message and the answer turned out to be a hamstring.

But then the video makes the move that earns the whole piece, and this is the bit I’d want you to notice.

It’s not the three. It’s the sequence.

Twenty-one, then sixteen, then three. Three consecutive reductions, in the same direction, across three weeks.

And here’s why that matters, and it’s the principle of the day.

One data point has many explanations. A trend has fewer.

Any single snap count can be explained by a game plan. A game plan can produce twenty-one one week and three another, because opponents differ. But three consecutive declines, each one smaller than the last, requires either three coincidences in a row or one cause that persists.

And the second is simpler.

Which is the same reasoning I used a week ago on something else. When a team responds differently to the same situation, the situation isn’t what changed. Here it’s the mirror of that. When a number moves the same way three times, the explanation that moves with it is the one to believe.

So the video is right to say we should not turn three snaps into a definitive conclusion, and also right to say we should not pretend three consecutive reductions from twenty-one to sixteen to three are meaningless.

Both. At once. Which is the thing I keep asking sources to do and which about one in eight of them manages.

Now the green dot, because this closes a thread I’ve been running since late October and the full chain is now complete.

The green dot is the sticker on the helmet of the defender with the radio in it. One man per side, per snap. He receives the call from the sideline and relays it.

Here is the whole sequence, which took five separate sources and three weeks to assemble.

DeMarvion Overshown started the season wearing it. He hurt his hamstring. This rookie took it over. Then against Philadelphia, Shemar James took it, with Overshown back on a limited workload and rotating.

And Brian Schottenheimer’s stated reason was that he praised James’s communication and command of the defense.

Which is a complete and reasonable explanation, and I said so at the time. A coach needs confidence that calls are arriving, and a signal-caller shouldn’t be your best player if your best player needs rest, because then every rotation becomes a communication change.

But here’s the observation this video makes that I had completely missed, and it’s the best thing in it.

Losing the headset did not have to mean losing playing time.

In fact the opposite. Without the burden of organising ten other men, a young linebacker should be freer to play his own position. Dallas could keep using his physical tools while asking him to do less of the thinking around him.

And Schottenheimer said exactly that himself, at his press conference on the thirtieth of October. He said moving the communication duties to James could free this man up.

Free him up. Those were the words. And then the snaps went from twenty-one to sixteen to three.

So here’s the thing that makes this story worth nine minutes, and it’s a tell in its own right, applied to a coach rather than a headline.

A stated reason predicts a consequence, and you can check whether the consequence arrived.

The stated reason was that removing the headset would free him to focus on his own assignment. The predicted consequence of that is the same snaps or more, played better. The actual consequence was eighty-five percent fewer snaps.

Which doesn’t make the stated reason a lie. Coaches say true things that turn out differently all the time, and I’ve spent a month insisting on that distinction. But it does mean the explanation given on the thirtieth of October no longer accounts for what happened afterwards, and something else is now doing the work.

And the video says precisely that. Those comments help make sense of the staff’s thinking, but they do not fully explain why he played only three snaps against Indianapolis.

Now the something else, because Schottenheimer actually named it in the same press conference.

He praised the rookie’s work against the run, and identified the passing game as an area where the linebacker was having difficulty.

Good against the run. Struggling in coverage.

And this is where I can add the mechanism, because I built it three weeks ago from a different direction and it explains everything.

A linebacker’s hardest problem is that a play does not stay the thing it looked like.

The offense presents a formation. The linebacker reads it. And then a man moves, or a back releases, or a tight end who looked like a blocker slips out, and the assignment he just committed to becomes the wrong one.

So the job has two halves that pull against each other. Attacking downhill against the run requires decisiveness, which means committing early. Covering a man requires patience, which means not committing until you know.

A young linebacker who is good against the run and struggling in coverage is usually a man whose first instinct is to come forward, and that instinct is correct about forty percent of the time and catastrophic the rest.

Which is the hardest thing in football to coach, because you are asking a man to be aggressive and patient on the same snap and to know which one this snap requires in about a quarter of a second.

And I said three weeks ago, about a different young defender, that what I’d watch is how he handles a tight end who shows him a run look and then slips out for a pass. That’s a tough read for a young linebacker who wants to come downhill.

Same problem. Same position. And now we know it’s the specific thing the staff has identified.

Now the Indianapolis detail, which is the strongest piece of evidence in the video and which the author is right to emphasise.

Overshown was inactive for that game.

So the man ahead of him wasn’t there. That’s an opening. And his participation dropped anyway, while Shemar James and the linebacker rendered as D. Winters handled most of the inside snaps.

That’s the bit that moves this from a rotation story to a hierarchy story. An injury to the man above you is the single clearest opportunity a backup gets, and he got three snaps.

And I’d note, because it closes another thread, that the man rendered as D. Winters is somebody I flagged six days ago as a linebacker I couldn’t place who had Dallas’s first interception of the season against Green Bay and had just returned from a calf injury as a full participant.

So the two men who played inside against Indianapolis were a rookie I labelled as probable three weeks ago and a man I flagged as unidentifiable six days ago, ahead of a third-round pick whose name I still can’t spell.

Which tells you something about how thin the public record on this team’s linebacker room actually is.

Now the thing I want to add, because the video raises it and then doesn’t quite finish it, and it’s the sharpest football idea available here.

The video says a role should not become so predictable that it gives the opposing offense an easy clue. If he only enters when Dallas expects a run, an opponent could respond by testing him with a different assignment.

That’s exactly right and it’s the thing I’ve been saying all month in a dozen different places, so let me name it properly.

A specialist is a tell.

Eleven men is zero-sum. Every substitution is information. If a defense puts a particular linebacker on the field only in short yardage, then his presence announces short yardage, and an offense with a tendency chart will throw at him the moment he appears.

Which is the exact mirror of the argument I made three weeks ago about Dallas’s offense. A sixth offensive lineman costs you a receiver, so heavy personnel announces itself, and the only way to stop it announcing itself is to throw out of it.

Same principle, opposite side of the ball. A defense wins by making one look mean two things. A substitution that only ever means one thing has handed the information away for free.

So the problem Dallas has with this player is genuinely hard, and I want to state it as the dilemma it is rather than as a criticism.

If you use him only where he’s strong, you’ve told the offense what’s coming. If you use him everywhere, you’ve exposed the thing he can’t do yet. And if you don’t use him at all, he doesn’t develop.

Three options and all three cost something. That’s not a coaching failure. That’s a third-round pick who isn’t ready, which is the normal condition of most third-round picks in November.

And the video’s proposed answer is the right shape, and it labels itself correctly as a possibility rather than a plan. A limited package with a clearly defined assignment. Short yardage or selected pressure calls.

Pressure calls are the cleverer half of that, and here’s why nobody says it.

A blitz is the one situation where a linebacker’s coverage weakness stops mattering, because he isn’t covering anybody. He’s rushing. So sending him is a way to get a run-strong, coverage-limited linebacker onto the field in a passing situation without exposing the thing he can’t do.

Which solves the tell problem too, because now his presence means two things rather than one.

That’s a real solution to a real problem and it took the video a sentence and me a paragraph, and it’s the most useful thing in here.

Now the part where I want to be fair in both directions, because this video does it and it’s the hardest thing to get right.

It says the Cowboys were justified in changing responsibilities when another player earned them. James and Winters should be on the field if they are executing more reliably. Competition for the rotation is part of the job. And it does not mean handing him snaps because of where he was drafted.

That is the correct position and almost nobody holds it, because draft position creates an entitlement in the mind of a fan base that it does not create on a field.

And then it says the other half, which is just as true. Development cannot depend entirely on an injury opening a spot. A young player needs opportunities to show progress and the staff needs ways to evaluate that progress in situations that make sense.

Both halves. Neither used to cancel the other.

And I’d add the only resolution I know of, which is that both obligations are real and they belong to different people.

The player’s job is to earn it. The staff’s job is to build a situation where earning it is possible.

Neither side can do the other’s work, which is the video’s own line and it’s a good one.
