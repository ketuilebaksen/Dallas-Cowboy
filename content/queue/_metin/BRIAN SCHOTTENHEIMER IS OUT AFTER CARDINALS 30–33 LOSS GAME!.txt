Here’s why I was asking. A week ago Dallas rushed for two hundred and four yards using a sixth offensive lineman. Eleven men on offense is zero-sum. Adding a sixth lineman means subtracting a receiver. So heavy personnel announces itself, and the only way to stop it announcing itself is to throw out of it. And I said this. I was expecting one of two outcomes. Either Dallas never throws from heavy personnel, in which case it’s a tell and Arizona’s tendency chart ate them alive. Or Dallas throws from it, in which case the grouping is honest and the defense has a real problem.

The actual answer is a third thing I hadn’t considered. Dallas threw out of heavy personnel, and the grouping still announced itself, because they did it with both of their best receivers on the bench.

Think about what the defense sees. Extra bodies up front. No Lamb. No Pickens. Second down near the two-minute warning.

That formation says this is not a real passing play even while it is one. The worst of both worlds, because you’ve paid the full cost of heavy personnel and received none of the benefit.

And here’s the mechanism, because I don’t think the video quite gets to it.

A play-action fake borrows its credibility from the situation, not just from the formation.

Play-action works because a run is plausible. The defense has to honour the handoff because the handoff would make sense. And what makes a run make sense late in a game is usually the clock. Running keeps the clock moving. That’s half of why anybody runs it in the fourth quarter.

Now put the two-minute warning in front of it. The clock stops there automatically. The video spots this and it’s the best observation in fourteen minutes. The clock was already going to stop, which means Dallas had a free opportunity to attack through the air without paying the clock cost it would pay anywhere else in the game.

So the run had no clock value on that snap. Which means a run fake had no credibility on that snap. Which means the play-action was not a lie the defense had to consider. It was free information.

The general principle. A fake only works when the thing you’re faking would have been a sensible decision. The defense doesn’t read the fake, it reads the situation, and the situation said throw.

So Dallas ran a play designed to look like a run, in a moment where a run was pointless, having removed the two men who make a throw dangerous.

And the diagnostic. Ask whether the reports are independent or merely simultaneous.

Real convergence carries the same sourcing. Three outlets with a genuine story will all point back to something. A document, a named reporter, a press conference, a club statement. You can trace all three back to one checkable origin.

Manufactured convergence has no origin at all. Each report cites a different pile of opinion, or cites nothing, and when you trace them back they don’t meet anywhere. They converge at the top, in the title, and nowhere underneath.

And once you see that, you know what the convergence is evidence about. It’s evidence about demand. Three channels published the same false claim within three days because a team blew a fourteen-point fourth-quarter lead and a great many people would like to read that somebody has been fired. The titles are not independent reports of an event. They are three independent responses to the same appetite.

Now the blitz, because the video identifies a real problem and I can add the geometry.

The criticism is that Dallas had no good answer to Arizona’s pressure and kept going back to bubble screens and quick throws to CeeDee Lamb, which stopped working because the defense anticipated them. And the video’s list of better answers is correct. Quick slants, crossing routes, screens away from the pressure, protection adjustments.

Here’s the mechanism that explains why that list is right and the bubble screen is wrong.

Pressure takes defenders out of the middle of the field. A bubble screen is the one concept that doesn’t go there.

Extra men rushing means fewer men covering, and the men who leave are leaving from somewhere. Usually that somewhere is the middle, because that’s where linebackers live and linebackers are the cheapest blitzers. So a blitz creates space behind it. Not beside it. Behind.

A slant goes behind it. A crossing route goes behind it. A dig goes behind it. All of those attack the ground the blitzer vacated, which is why they punish pressure.

A bubble screen goes sideways. It attacks the edge, it needs two or three blockers out in front, and it needs the receiver to have room to turn upfield. None of those things are created by a blitz. And if the defense has overloaded the side the ball is going to, the screen runs directly into men who were already there.

So the bubble screen is not a bad play. It’s a play whose geometry has nothing to do with the problem it was being used to solve.

And the video’s framing is right. A blitz isn’t automatically a problem when the offense has a reliable answer. It becomes a problem when the defense knows what’s coming and the offense keeps giving it the same opportunities. Same structure as the heavy personnel point. The issue isn’t the concept. It’s that the concept had become predictable, and a predictable concept against a prepared defense is a snap you’ve already lost.

One more criticism in the video I’m going to refuse to adjudicate. It says Dallas should have used its timeout before the final field goal attempt rather than letting the kick proceed.

A timeout is a currency with two uses. It stops the clock, and it buys you a conversation. Spending it on one means you can’t spend it on the other. And whether you want the clock stopped before a kick depends on the score, the time remaining, and whose ball it is afterwards. If you’re kicking to tie, you may want the clock to run, because every second you burn is a second the opponent doesn’t get.

I don’t have the score at that moment and I don’t have the clock. Given that the game went to overtime, that kick was probably the tying attempt at the end of regulation, and if it was, letting the clock run may well have been correct.

Ask whether the sentence names an action or a trait.

I’m a problem solver names a trait. Traits aren’t performed, they’re claimed, and the only evidence for a trait is a pile of anecdotes selected by the person claiming it. There’s nothing to check on Sunday, because no snap of football will ever confirm or refute it.

So the complete tool now has three steps. Invert the adverb and you learn nothing. Invert the subject and you learn who’s accepting it. Ask action or trait and you learn whether anything was actually promised.

Now the last section, and it’s the most responsible thing in the video.

Yesterday I gave you tell 32, the headline reporting a demand as the event, and the diagnostic was to ask who would have to act for the headline to be true.

Today’s video actually does that work. It says Jerry Jones has to consider whether the current leadership can deliver the improvement this team needs, and then goes through what he’d have to weigh. Changing head coaches during a season can disrupt the locker room and affect continuity. He has to evaluate who would take over, whether the current staff contains a viable alternative, and whether a replacement could realistically improve performance.

So let me add the mechanism that makes it true, because it’s the most underrated fact about in-season firings.

Firing a coach in November doesn’t get you a better coach. It gets you his coordinator.

The men you would actually want are employed. They’re coaching other teams, or they’re in television waiting for January, and none of them are available on a Tuesday in week nine. So an in-season change isn’t a choice between the current coach and the best available coach. It’s a choice between the current coach and somebody already in that building, who was hired by the current coach, to run the current coach’s system.

Which means the honest question is never should he be fired. It’s whether there is a man in that building who would do it better between now and January. If the answer is no, the decision is about January, and if it’s about January, there is no reason on earth to make it in November.

And the counterweight, which the video also gets. Keeping him without demanding meaningful improvement would be equally difficult to defend. That’s sunk cost. An organisation that hired a man recently has an interest in his succeeding, and that interest can quietly become a reason not to look at the evidence.

So both failure modes are live. Firing him in anger over one collapse is reacting to variance. Keeping him without conditions is protecting a decision. And what distinguishes an organisation from a mood is whether it can name, in advance, what improvement would look like.
