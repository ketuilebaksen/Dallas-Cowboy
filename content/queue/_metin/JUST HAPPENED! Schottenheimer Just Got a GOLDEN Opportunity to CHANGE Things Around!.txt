Alright. Now the part I’m actually interested in.

Yesterday I made what I thought was the strongest argument of my season. I’d gotten a new number: Dak Prescott’s yards per completion is just over ten, which is the lowest of his entire career. And I built a whole thesis on it.

I said that number is the shape of this offense, not a judgment on anybody’s arm. I said if every completion is ten yards, a touchdown drive requires seven or eight consecutive clean plays, which isn’t an offense, it’s a tightrope. I said explosive plays are the thing that shortens the rope. And I said my top watch item for Monday night was now yards per completion, because I don’t need a win to see progress, I need completions that travel.

Today’s analyst argues the exact opposite.

Here’s what he says. Five easy yards can help this offense more than another difficult throw downfield. Dallas needs to give itself a chance to build a drive before chasing the big finish. Let Prescott get the ball out. Let receivers make something happen after the catch.

So he wants it shorter. I wanted it longer. Same team, same two games, same week, opposite prescriptions.

And I’ve sat with it, and he’s more right than I was, and here’s why, because the resolution is genuinely interesting.

My mistake was treating an average like a target.

Yards per completion is a mean. And there are two completely different ways to produce a mean of ten yards, and they are not equally healthy.

The first way is that everything is ten yards. Every completion is a ten-yard throw. Nothing short, nothing long, a flat distribution centered on ten.

The second way is that most of your completions are four and five yards, and then two or three times a game somebody catches one for forty.

Those two offenses have identical yards per completion and they are not remotely the same football team. The second one is how every good passing offense in the league actually works. The distribution is bimodal. Lots of cheap, easy, high-percentage completions, punctuated by a small number of throws that travel.

What’s actually broken is the flat distribution. A steady diet of contested fifteen-yard throws is the worst offense you can build, because those throws are the hardest ones to complete, the most interception-prone, and the easiest to defend. And they are precisely what a two-high safety defense is designed to take away.

So when he says take the five yards and when I say I want completions that travel, we are not in conflict. We are both describing the same bimodal distribution from different ends. And the thing we are both against, without either of us having said it clearly, is the middle.

And then there’s the second half of the resolution, which is the part I actually got wrong, and it’s about order.

You cannot start with the deep ball. You have to earn it.

I built a thing earlier this season I called the Pickens gravity cascade. The argument was that without completed deep balls, safeties stop respecting the top of the field, they come down, CeeDee Lamb stops getting one-on-ones, and the field compresses from the inside out.

That cascade is real. But I only ever described it running in one direction, and today’s video made me notice that it runs both ways.

Running downhill, which is how I built it: no deep completions, so the safeties come down, so everything compresses.

Running uphill, which is how you fix it: the run game and the quick passing game force the safeties to care about what’s happening in front of them, which pulls their attention and eventually their feet forward, which is the only thing that ever creates the deep ball in the first place.

So the short game is not the opposite of the deep ball. The short game is the price of the deep ball.

And against Vic Fangio specifically, with two safeties sitting deep and a light box, the deep ball is the one thing on the field you absolutely cannot take by asking for it. I actually half-conceded this yesterday when I moved the deep shot down my own watch list for this opponent. Today I’m finishing the concession. His sequencing is right and mine was backwards.

Which means I have to upgrade my own watch item, and the upgraded version is better.

Yards per completion was the wrong number. Not useless, but wrong, because an average can’t tell you which of those two offenses you’re looking at.

What I want instead is two numbers that describe the distribution.

Number one. How many completions travel more than twenty yards. Not the average. The count. Two of those changes a football game and doesn’t move an average much at all.

Number two. Average third-down distance. Because that’s the number that tells you whether the short game is working as a foundation or as a substitute.

And that second one brings me to the most useful thing in today’s video, which the analyst says in passing and doesn’t develop, so let me develop it.

He says he wants the run to help create shorter third downs. And he says five easy yards can help this offense.

Here’s the key that makes both of those true, and it resolves the apparent contradiction with everything I said yesterday.

A five-yard completion on first down is progress. A five-yard completion on third and eight is a punt.

The exact same throw, with the exact same execution, is either the foundation of a drive or the end of one, and the only difference is the down and the distance.

Which gives me a real hypothesis about this offense, and it’s the first mechanical explanation I’ve had for the third-down problem.

What if the distribution is flat across downs?

What if Dallas is running roughly the same ten-yard menu on first down that it runs on third and eight? Because that would produce exactly the box score we keep seeing. On first down, a ten-yard menu is fine. It gets you to second and short sometimes and second and seven mostly. On third and eight, a ten-yard menu converts when it’s executed perfectly and fails when anything goes slightly wrong.

Five of thirteen on third down against Green Bay. Thirteen third downs, which as I told you is a normal number of possessions, not a blowout artifact.

An offense with an identity has a different menu for each down, because the entire point of an identity is knowing what you are in a specific situation. An offense still finding an identity, which is what the offensive coordinator said in week eight, has one menu and runs it everywhere.

I can’t prove that from here. I don’t have the down-by-down distribution. But it’s a real hypothesis, it’s testable with information that exists, and it’s the first thing I’ve heard that explains the third-down number mechanically rather than just describing it.

Now the second disagreement, which is about Fangio, and I lose this one too, though less badly.

Yesterday I told you what a Fangio defense does. Two safeties deep, light box, rush four, don’t blitz much, play sound coverage behind it, bend but don’t break.

Today’s analyst says this. Vic Fangio has seen enough football to recognize when an offense is struggling with pressure. I wouldn’t assume he’ll approach Dallas exactly the way he approaches everybody else. If extra rushers have been creating problems, why wouldn’t he test that again? Schottenheimer has to anticipate it. Hoping the Eagles leave the weakness alone is not a game plan.

And he’s right, and the reason he’s right is a distinction I should have made explicitly.

A tendency is a prior, not a prediction.

When I tell you a coach plays two-high and rushes four, I am telling you what he does against the average opponent, averaged across a season. That’s a base rate. It’s what you should believe before you look at the specific matchup.

And then you look at the specific matchup, and good coaches deviate from their own base rates precisely when the opponent gives them a reason. That’s not inconsistency. That’s the job. A coordinator who runs his tendencies regardless of who he’s playing isn’t a coordinator, he’s a thermostat.

So the honest version of my preview is this. Fangio’s design is two-high and four-man rush, and that remains the most likely shape of Monday night, and if Dallas has a specific, visible, exploitable problem with extra rushers, then the probability of seeing extra rushers goes up from whatever his season rate is.

And hoping he leaves it alone is not a plan. That line is correct and it’s the kind of sentence I should have written myself.

But now I want to interrogate the premise underneath it, because there’s something genuinely strange here, and working through it has produced the best single idea I’ve had all week.

Does Dallas actually struggle against pressure?

Because yesterday I was handed a number that says the opposite. Dak Prescott has the best sack-avoidance rate in the league, at two point seven seven percent, after I put the decimal point back where it belonged.

So you have an analyst saying this offense struggles with pressure, and a statistic saying this quarterback is the hardest man in football to sack. Those look like they can’t both be true.

They’re both true. And the way they’re both true is the thing I want you to walk away with today.

A sack is not the only price of pressure. It’s just the only one that shows up in a box score.

When an extra rusher comes and the quarterback gets the ball out in one point eight seconds to a receiver four yards downfield, nothing bad appears anywhere in the statistics. No sack. No hit. No incompletion. A completion, in fact. It looks like a win.

What actually happened is that the offense paid for that pressure in yards instead of paying for it in sacks. The route progression got truncated. The intended target never got looked at. The play that was called did not happen, and a cheaper play happened in its place.

So now put the three observations next to each other.

A league-best sack-avoidance rate.

A career-low yards per completion.

An analyst saying this offense struggles with pressure.

Those are not three findings. They are one behavior described three ways. The ball comes out early. That produces the sack rate, it produces the yards per completion, and it is what struggling with pressure actually looks like for a quarterback who is very good at not getting hit.

Which has a direct consequence for Monday night that I don’t think anybody else is going to tell you.

If Fangio sends extra rushers, Dak Prescott probably will not get sacked. He is excellent at this and the ball will come out.

And it will come out five yards downfield on third and eight.

The pressure will work and the sack column will be empty, and if you are watching the sack column to judge whether the pass rush beat the protection, you will come away thinking Dallas handled it fine while Dallas punts six times.

So that’s my third watch item now, and it’s a specific one. On third down with more than six yards to go, where does the ball get caught? Behind the sticks, or at them? That one answer tells you whether the pressure is winning, regardless of what the sack total says.

Now, the best piece of coaching language in this video, which the analyst uses correctly and which explains something I’ve been describing for three weeks without having the right word for it.

He says: cover the sticks, tackle before the first down, and avoid the mistake that extends the drive.

Cover the sticks. Let me explain exactly what that means, because it’s the mechanic behind the pattern I keep pointing at.

In zone coverage, every underneath defender has a landmark and a depth. And the depth is a coaching decision, not an accident. A defender dropping to a curl zone has to settle somewhere, and where he settles determines what’s available in front of him.

Now here’s the thing. On third and seven, the line to gain is seven yards away. If your underneath defenders settle at five yards, you have built a defense in which every single completion in front of you is a first down. The coverage worked. The play was defended. The receiver was blanketed. And the sticks moved.

Covering the sticks means your depth is set by the distance, not by habit. On third and seven you settle at eight. On third and twelve you settle at thirteen. The line to gain, not your comfortable landmark.

And this is the mechanism behind the thing I’ve been calling the third-down problem all month. The source describes the symptom perfectly: Dallas cannot keep giving quarterbacks another chance after winning the first two plays. Winning two plays means nothing if the opponent converts the next one.

That’s not an effort problem and it’s not a talent problem. It’s a depth-discipline problem, and depth discipline is one of the most teachable things in football. It’s a week of walkthroughs. You can genuinely fix it in seven days, which is more than you can say for almost anything else wrong with this team.

So if Dallas’s third-down defense is better Monday night, that’s the likeliest reason, and it would be a real sign that the coaching is reaching the players, which is a question I set a falsifiable condition on two days ago.

Now let me put the injuries together, because there’s a pattern and I want to handle it honestly.

Overshown, hamstring, five games. The cornerback, hamstring, four games. Drew Shelton, the rookie offensive lineman, still sidelined with a hamstring. Saquon Barkley on the other side returning from a hamstring. DeVonta Smith missing Thursday’s practice with a hamstring.

That’s five hamstrings across two rosters in one week in late October.

Now, what can you conclude from that? And the honest answer is almost nothing about any one team, but something real about the calendar.

Soft tissue injuries cluster in late October for a physiological reason. The accumulated workload of a training camp plus seven or eight games plus a month of short practice weeks arrives all at once, and a hamstring is the first thing to fail under accumulated load, because it’s the muscle that absorbs deceleration.

And I’ll repeat the thing I keep repeating, because it’s the most misunderstood mechanic in injury coverage. Hamstrings don’t fail from running. They fail from reacting. The unplanned change of direction. The sudden acceleration from a dead stop when a back breaks the wrong way.

Which is why limited practice means limited, and why the individual-drill versus team-drill distinction on a practice report tells you more than the words full or limited ever will.

What I will not do is tell you Dallas has a training staff problem. Three hamstrings is not a sample, it’s a Tuesday in late October, and two of them are on the other team’s injury report.

One more Dallas injury, and the source makes a smarter point about it than I expected.

KaVontae Turpin is expected to miss time with a rib injury, and the analyst notes it affects more than one part of the team, because Dallas loses a threat who creates field position in the return game and quick yards on offense.

Let me add the consequence, because I think it’s worse for this specific team than for most.

Field position is the hidden input to every drive statistic. Points per drive, which I’ve been using all season and warning you about all season, has a blind spot for where the drive started. A team that starts at its own twenty instead of its own thirty-five needs fifteen more yards per drive to arrive at the same place.

Now put that on top of the tightrope. If your offense averages ten yards per completion, fifteen extra yards is one and a half additional completions on every single possession, which is one and a half additional chances to take a penalty, miss a throw, or lose a down.

So losing a returner costs a short-passing offense more than it costs an explosive one. An offense that hits forty-yard plays doesn’t care much where the drive started. An offense grinding out tens cares enormously.

That’s not incompetence. It’s just how a thin roster feels in week eight.
