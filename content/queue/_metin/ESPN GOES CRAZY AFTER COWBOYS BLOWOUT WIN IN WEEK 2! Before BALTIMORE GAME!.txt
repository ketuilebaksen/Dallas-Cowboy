How do you stop Derrick Henry?

Because this is the matchup, and honestly, for this specific Dallas defense, it might be the worst possible one.

Let's start with what makes him different, because it isn't speed and it isn't elusiveness in the traditional sense.

It's yards after contact.

Most running backs are trying to avoid you. Henry is a big, powerful, downhill runner whose whole game is built on the idea that the first man who touches him isn't going to bring him down. He pounds between the tackles, he churns through arm tackles, and he keeps his legs moving after contact in a way that turns a two-yard gain into a seven-yard gain.

And he catches screens when a defense overplays the box, which means you can't just crowd the line and forget about him.

He's been the lead back somewhere for a very long time, carrying heavy workloads deep into his career, and evaluators have spent years marveling at the durability.

Now here's why that specific skill set is a nightmare for the Dallas defense right now.

Go back to our numbers. Against the Giants, Dallas allowed a hundred sixty-five rushing yards. Against Washington, by subtraction, roughly a hundred eighty.

And the specific failures we identified were missed tackles in space and linebackers arriving out of control.

A running back whose entire game is defeating the first tackler, against a defense whose problem is tackling. That's the matchup. That's the whole game in one sentence.

So let me explain what actually stops a runner like this, because it's not one guy.

It's gang tackling.

Against a normal back, one defender arriving in good position ends the play. Against Henry, one defender arriving in good position slows him down enough for the second and third defenders to finish it. The first man's job isn't to make the tackle. It's to get his hands on him, get low, and take away his legs, so the cavalry can arrive.

That requires effort from everybody, on every snap, including players on the backside of the play who look like they have nothing to do. The defender who jogs on a run to the opposite side is the reason a seven-yard gain becomes a thirty-yard gain.

And that kind of pursuit is exhausting. Which brings me to the thing that scares me most.

Remember the Giants game. New York held the ball for over thirty-six minutes. I explained at the time that a defense on the field that long stops tackling properly, because technique is the first thing to go when legs go.

Baltimore's entire offensive identity is built to do exactly that to you. Run the ball, control the clock, wear you down, and then let Henry break one in the fourth quarter against a defense with nothing left.

That's not a bad game plan. That's the plan they'd run against anybody. It just happens to attack Dallas exactly where Dallas has already been broken once.

Now the tackling technique itself, because it genuinely matters against this runner.

Against a big back, going high is how you get run over and how you end up on a highlight reel. The answer is leverage. Get low, hit at or below the thigh, wrap and squeeze, and take away his base. You are not trying to knock him backward. Nobody knocks him backward. You're trying to take his legs out from under him and hold on until help comes.

For a young linebacker room that already had a missed tackle problem in Week One, that is a very demanding ask.

And there's a psychological element to facing a back like this that people underestimate.

Tackling a powerful runner hurts. Not in a dramatic way, but in the accumulating way that matters over sixty minutes. Every collision costs something, and a defense that has been hit repeatedly by the same man starts, very slightly and very humanly, to arrive a fraction of a second later, a little higher, a little less willing.

That's the real weapon of a punishing run game. It isn't the yardage on any single carry. It's what carry number eighteen feels like compared to carry number three.

Good defenses fight that by rotating bodies, keeping fresh legs in the game, and by leaning on the mental side. Christian Parker has a real defensive line rotation to lean on, and that rotation matters more this week than it has all season.

Which means the depth players we've been talking about all month, the Otito Ogbonnias of this roster, actually have a job on Sunday. Not a glamorous one. Just keeping the starters fresh enough to still tackle in the fourth quarter.

And here's the trap, the thing that makes this game genuinely difficult to coach.

If Dallas overcommits to stopping Henry, Baltimore has the counterpunch ready.

That's what play-action is for. Sell the handoff, let the linebackers and safeties take two steps downhill toward Henry, and throw behind them. The more scared you are of the run, the wider those windows get.

So the answer can't just be bring everybody to the line. The answer is that the men up front have to win their individual matchups so the back seven doesn't have to cheat.

And there's one more strategic layer that I think is the most underrated part of this game, and it has nothing to do with the defense.

The best way to keep Derrick Henry off the field is to keep your own offense on it.

That sounds like a cliché, and it isn't. Against a team built on clock control, possessions are the currency. If Dallas goes three and out twice in the first quarter, Baltimore gets two extra drives, which is twelve or fifteen extra carries, which is exactly how a power run game breaks a defense.

But if the Dallas offense plays the way it did against Washington, twenty-six of thirty-one with four touchdowns, and sustains drives, then Henry is standing on the sideline with his helmet on, watching. A back who touches the ball fourteen times is a very different problem than a back who touches it twenty-four times.

And there's a second effect that people forget. Scoreboard pressure changes a run game. If Dallas builds a two-score lead, Baltimore has to start throwing to catch up, which takes the ball out of Henry's hands entirely and puts Lamar Jackson in obvious passing situations, where Von Miller actually matters.

So the offense isn't just the other half of this game. It's part of the run defense plan.

That's the way I'd frame Sunday if I were on that staff. Score early, stay on schedule, and make Baltimore play a game it doesn't want to play.
