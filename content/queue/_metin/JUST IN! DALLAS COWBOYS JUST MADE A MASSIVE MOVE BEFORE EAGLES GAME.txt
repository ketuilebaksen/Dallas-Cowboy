Tell number twenty-three. The transaction-shaped word with no object.

Move. Decision. Shake-up. Development. Change. Situation.

Those are nouns that imply something happened without committing to what. And they are more sophisticated than the fabrications I’ve been showing you all week, because they are almost impossible to be wrong about.

Here’s the difference. Yesterday’s headline said trade. A trade is a specific legal event. Assets move in both directions, the league office processes it, and you can check whether it occurred. So when a headline says trade and no trade exists, the headline is false and I can prove it.

Today’s headline says move.

Did Dallas make a move this week? Well, yes. They waived Caelen Carson. They signed Juanyeh Thomas to the active roster. They signed Kyle Trask to the practice squad. They lost Joe Milton the Third to Philadelphia. Moves happened.

So the headline is technically defensible, and completely misleading about the contents of this video, at the same time.

And that’s the trick. The word was chosen precisely because it’s vague enough to survive a complaint. There is no possible state of the world in which an NFL team has made no moves. The claim cannot fail.

Now, those of you who’ve been here a couple of weeks will recognize where this goes, because it’s the same engine that drives half this list.

Two days ago I gave you tell nineteen, the headline that supplies an emotion. Furious. Sinister. Shocked. And I told you emotion is the cheapest content that exists because nobody can check a mood.

Tell twenty-three is the same principle applied to the noun instead of the adjective.

Tell nineteen was the unfalsifiable adjective. Tell twenty-three is the unfalsifiable noun.

And the test is one question. Does the word have truth conditions?

Meaning: can you describe the state of the world that would make this headline false? Dallas traded a second-round pick is false if they didn’t. Dallas made a massive move is false if... nothing. There’s no answer. Which means the word isn’t reporting anything. It’s a shape where a fact should be.

So whenever you see move, development, decision or situation in a headline with no object attached, finish the sentence out loud. Dallas made a massive move to do what, involving whom? If the video can’t answer, the headline wasn’t a claim. It was a silhouette.

Now the number, and this is the heart of today.

The source says Dallas ran the ball just once over thirty-seven plays.

One run. Thirty-seven plays.

Let me put that in context. Across the league, teams run the football somewhere in the neighborhood of forty percent of the time. One carry in thirty-seven snaps is under three percent.

And before I do anything else with it, I want to credit what this source does immediately after stating it, because it’s the exact discipline I’ve been preaching for two weeks.

It says: that figure needs to be understood within its original game context.

Yes. Thank you. That is the right sentence and almost nobody writes it.

Because here’s the problem with one run in thirty-seven plays, and it’s the same problem I warned you about on Thursday when I was handed a rushing differential.

Run-pass balance is the single most game-script-contaminated statistic in football.

If Dallas was down seventeen to nothing at halftime, as they were at Lambeau Field, then throwing on thirty-six of thirty-seven snaps in the second half is not a scandal. It’s correct football. A trailing team throws because an incomplete pass stops the clock and a two-yard run does not. Refusing to run while down three scores in the fourth quarter is competence, not cowardice.

So the meaning of one-in-thirty-seven depends entirely on one piece of information this video does not give me: what was the score during those thirty-seven plays?

I don’t have it. So I can’t convict a play-caller on it, and I’m not going to.

But I can tell you this is now the single most important unanswered question about this offense, and it is answerable in about four minutes by anybody with a play-by-play sheet in front of them. That’s it. Four minutes. Pull the drive chart, find the thirty-seven-play window, read the score column.

And here’s why I care so much, because this number bears directly on a thing I have been circling for a month without being able to settle.

I have been telling you Dallas is thirty-first in the league in rushing yardage. Four hundred and fifty-six yards in six games. Seventy-six a game. Forty-eight at Lambeau.

And on Thursday I complained, correctly, that I only had the numerator. I said eight hundred and ninety-nine rushing yards allowed could be five and a half yards a carry on few attempts or three point nine on many attempts, and those are two completely different defenses producing the identical number. I called it the hidden denominator problem and I said I needed attempts.

Today a window into the denominator arrived, and it changes the shape of the question.

Here’s the distinction that makes it work, and I think it’s the cleanest thing I’ve come up with in a while.

Rushing rank measures outcome. Run rate measures intent.

Thirty-first in rushing yardage tells you what happened. It cannot tell you whether you can’t run or whether you don’t run, because both produce low yardage.

Run rate tells you what you chose. And choice is the thing you can actually hold a coach responsible for.

So the conditional conclusion, stated as a conditional because that’s all the evidence supports.

If that one-in-thirty-seven distribution reflects how this offense behaves in competitive game states, then being thirty-first in rushing is not primarily an offensive line failure. It’s a decision. And every argument about the run game this season has been pointed at the wrong people.

If that window came entirely out of a two-score deficit, then it’s an artifact, the offensive line conversation stands, and this number tells you nothing you didn’t know.

Those are two completely different football teams, and I can’t tell you which one Dallas is. What I can tell you is that this is the number to go find, and it’s the first time all season I’ve been able to say exactly what piece of evidence would settle the argument.

Now let me do the mechanism, because the source explains why the run game matters and does it well, and then I want to extend it in two directions it doesn’t go.

Here’s what it says. When a defense doesn’t respect the possibility of a handoff, linebackers can react more aggressively to the quarterback, defensive ends can focus on attacking upfield, and coverage defenders can concentrate on the passing game without worrying as much about run responsibilities.

All correct. Now let me price each one, because the numbers are bigger than people think.

Start with the defensive ends. An edge rusher who has to honor a run has to read his key off the snap, and reading costs him a fraction of a second and a step of angle. An edge rusher who knows it’s a pass gets to take the widest, deepest, most aggressive rush angle available immediately. That difference is maybe a tenth or two of a second on the rush, which doesn’t sound like anything until you remember that a pass rush needs about two and a half seconds to matter. Two tenths is roughly eight percent of the pocket. That is the margin between pressure and no pressure on a lot of snaps.

Now the linebackers, and this is the extension I think matters most, because it connects to something I gave you yesterday.

A linebacker who must honor the run has to read before he drops. Read his key, confirm it’s a pass, then turn and get depth. That read costs him two or three yards of coverage depth, every single snap, for free.

Yesterday I explained cover the sticks. On third and seven, if your underneath defenders settle at five yards instead of eight, every completion in front of you is a first down, no matter how well the coverage was played.

Put those two things together.

A defense that doesn’t have to honor the run gets its underneath depth for free. The linebackers drop at the snap instead of reading first, which buys them exactly the two or three yards that separate settling at five from settling at eight.

Which means the run game is how you buy third-down conversions.

Not by running on third down. By making linebackers read on every down, so that when third and seven arrives, they are two yards shallower than they want to be.

Dallas converted five of thirteen third downs at Lambeau Field. I have spent three videos describing that number and one video explaining the mechanism from the defensive side. This is the first time I’ve been able to connect it to the run game, and the connection is that opponents’ linebackers may be getting their depth for free all night.

And there’s a third extension the source doesn’t make, which is the most expensive one.

Play-action.

I’ve told you before that play-action doesn’t work because defenders are fooled. It works because defenders are paid to be aggressive against the run, and aggression has a direction. One hard step the wrong way by a linebacker is all a crossing route needs.

Play-action is, by most measures, the single highest-efficiency category of passing play in football. It generates more yards per attempt than almost anything else you can call.

And it requires a run threat to exist. Not a good one, necessarily. A credible one.

So an offense that runs once in thirty-seven plays has not only given up its run game. It has given up access to its best passing concept, because there is nothing behind the fake.

Which brings me to the Pickens question, and I think I can give you a defense of him that is fair without excusing him.

The complaint, as reported, has two parts, and the source correctly notes that they are different complaints that keep getting treated as one. That’s a sharp observation and it deserves amplifying.

Complaint one. He doesn’t block hard enough on running plays.

That one is completely legitimate and I’m not going to argue with it. Receivers have real responsibilities in the run game. A receiver who loses his block on the perimeter is the difference between a nine-yard gain and a forty-yard gain, because once a running back clears the first level, the only men left are the ones a receiver is supposed to be covering up. It’s on film, it’s measurable, and being your team’s most important weapon does not exempt you from it. The source says exactly that and it’s right.

Complaint two. He doesn’t sell the run convincingly enough on passing plays.

And this is where I want to defend him, specifically and mechanically.

You cannot sell a run you never run.

A play-action fake is a lie, and a lie only works if the truth is plausible. When a receiver sprints off the line and sticks his hands into a defender like it’s an outside-zone run, he is making an argument. And the argument is only persuasive if that team actually runs outside zone sometimes.

If you have run the football once in thirty-seven snaps, the defensive back across from him is not reading his hands. He’s read the box score. He has been told all week in a meeting room that this team does not run, and no amount of acting by a wide receiver overrides a scouting report.

So the second complaint is asking a player to manufacture credibility that the play-caller declined to purchase.

Which does not get him off the hook for the first complaint. Both things are true. He should block better on actual runs, and his fakes are worthless for reasons that have nothing to do with him.

And notice what that means for the criticism as a whole. One half of it is a fair coaching point about effort. The other half is a coaching point addressed to the wrong man.

Now, the public part, because that’s a separate question from the football.

Public criticism of a player by a head coach is a tool, and like every tool it has a price.

What it buys you is a signal. To the locker room, that standards apply to everybody including the stars. To the fan base, that somebody noticed. That’s worth something, and coaches who never do it end up with rosters where accountability is a rumor.

What it costs you is relationship, and more importantly it costs you credibility if it’s selective. And here’s the asymmetry that makes it dangerous.

A head coach can criticize a player in public. A player cannot criticize the head coach in public. Not without it becoming a much bigger story than the original complaint.

So the exchange is never symmetric. Which means selective public criticism reads as scapegoating even when the underlying football point is correct, because the man being criticized has no comparable way to respond.

And the source puts its finger on exactly the right line. There’s a difference between correcting a player’s performance and making that player feel as though he’s being blamed for a teamwide failure. Public accountability becomes counterproductive if it creates the impression that one player is being made the face of a problem involving multiple positions and coaching decisions.

So here’s my verdict, and it’s a split one.

The football point about blocking is probably correct and was worth making.

The delivery, in public, aimed at one receiver, in a week when this offense is thirty-first in rushing and its own coordinator has said it’s still finding an identity, was a mistake. Not because the criticism was unfair, but because of what it looks like next to everything else, and a head coach is responsible for what things look like. That’s part of the job description.

And one thing I want to say plainly, because I’ve been building a framework about this all week and today it applies directly.

You can hold a receiver accountable and still demand better play-calling from the head coach. The source says that too, in almost the same words, and it’s the correct frame. Those positions are not mutually exclusive. A team can have a receiver who blocks lazily and a play-caller who has abandoned the run, at the same time, and pretending you have to choose is how fan arguments go in circles for a month.

Now, two new pieces of information in this video that I need to deal with, and both of them land on me.

The first one is about Mike Pettine.

The source mentions, near the end, concerns about Mike Pettine’s reported absence from the practice sidelines, and Christian Parker’s explanations regarding the situation.

Now I have to be disciplined here, because I do not know why, and there are entirely benign explanations. A coach whose role is in the booth on game day genuinely may not need to be on the practice field. Analysts, advisers and consultants work different hours than position coaches. It could also be health, or travel, or a role that was defined narrowly on purpose. I’m not going to speculate, and I’d ask you not to either.

But I can say what it does to my own argument, and it refines it in a way I didn’t anticipate.

When Pettine was hired, I raised a two-cooks concern. Then I walked it back, because I learned their split was by function rather than by day, with Pettine in the booth and Parker on the sideline calling it. Then on Thursday an analyst raised the same worry from a different angle, which was not about the coaches disagreeing but about what reaches the players. And I set a falsifiable condition: if blown assignments and communication errors go down over three weeks, the arrangement worked.

Here’s the refinement. The install happens at practice.

Game-day diagnosis happens in the booth, and from the booth Pettine can be enormously valuable. He can see the whole field, identify what an offense is doing, and feed it down to the sideline between drives. That’s a real contribution and it’s the thing he was hired for.

But the teaching, the repetitions, the walkthrough where a linebacker learns that on third and seven he settles at eight yards and not at five, happens on Wednesday and Thursday on a practice field.

So his absence from that field, whatever the reason, narrows the mechanism by which the hire can help. It makes it a game-day upgrade rather than an install upgrade.

And those are different sizes of thing. A game-day upgrade is worth real points. An install upgrade is worth a season. If what Dallas bought is the first one, that’s fine and useful and smaller than it was sold as.

Which sharpens my condition rather than retracting it. If blown assignments drop in the next three weeks, something is working. If they don’t, I’d now expect the reason to be that the problem lives on the practice field and the help is in the booth.

Alright. Now the leadership argument, which is the back half of this video, and I want to engage with it properly because there’s a real idea in it and also an unfalsifiable one.

The real idea first, and the source states it better than I would have.

There is a difference between being a good offensive mind and being an effective head coach.

A coordinator can spend most of his attention on one side of the football, developing schemes and preparing specific matchups. A head coach has to oversee the whole operation, manage the staff, establish standards, and make sure offense, defense and special teams are pointed at the same objective.

That’s correct and it’s the honest frame for evaluating a first-year head coach, and it fits exactly inside the four-ledger framework I gave you Friday. The head coach owns the opening plan, the penalty count, the install and the standards. The coordinator owns the identity and the route distribution. The front office owns the roster. The quarterback owns his throws and reads.

And it raises a question that I have been unable to answer all month, which I’m going to state plainly because it matters enormously and I refuse to guess.

Who actually calls the plays in Dallas?

Klayton Adams is the offensive coordinator. Brian Schottenheimer is the head coach and came up as an offensive mind. And this video talks about demanding better play-calling from the head coach, which implies he’s involved.

If Schottenheimer calls the plays, then the one-in-thirty-seven number is his and the run-pass balance argument lands directly on him.

If Adams calls them, then it lands on Adams, and the head coach’s accountability is for the standard rather than the sequence.

I don’t know. Those are two completely different accountability pictures, and every argument about this offense is being conducted without settling it. If you know, tell me, and I’ll use it.

Now the unfalsifiable idea, because I have to be consistent.

Holmes argues that Schottenheimer’s performance has raised serious questions about whether he has the authority and leadership necessary to turn things around. And to its credit, this source pushes back immediately: that’s a strong accusation and we shouldn’t automatically assume the entire locker room has lost faith in its head coach.

Good. Because losing the locker room is one of the most unfalsifiable claims in all of sports.

Nobody can check it. It’s a claim about a collective internal state, in a building you can’t enter, about men who have every professional incentive to say nothing. It cannot be confirmed and it cannot be denied, which puts it in exactly the same family as furious and sinister. It’s the tell-nineteen problem at the organizational level.

So let me do what I’d want somebody to do, which is name what would actually count as evidence. Here are three things that are genuinely observable.

One, and this is the best one. Decompose the penalties.

Dallas committed eleven penalties for more than a hundred yards at Lambeau Field, and I have been using that number for a week as my central diagnostic. But I’ve been treating it as one thing and it is clearly two.

Illegal formation, false start, illegal shift, illegal motion, delay of game. Those are procedure penalties. They are what a new system produces while it’s settling, and they belong to the install.

Unnecessary roughness, unsportsmanlike conduct, post-whistle contact, taunting. Those are discipline penalties. They are what a team produces when it’s frustrated and nobody is being held to a standard, and they belong to the culture.

Same total. Completely different diagnoses. And they are listed by name in a penalty log that anybody can read.

So that’s my upgraded test, and it’s better than the one I had. Not eleven penalties. Which eleven.

Two, effort on film. Pursuit angles from the backside. Second-effort blocks. Whether anybody runs forty yards to a tackle that’s already been made. That’s visible, it’s judgeable, and it’s the oldest tell in coaching.

Three, whether the criticism flows in both directions. A head coach criticizing a receiver in public is normal. A locker room where players respond in public is not. So far, as far as I know, no player has said a word, including the one being criticized. That’s not evidence of harmony, but the absence of the loud version is at least the absence of one bad sign.

And the honest summary is that none of those three tells you about a locker room’s faith. They tell you about behavior, which is the only thing anybody outside a building can actually see.
