So here’s tell number twenty-two, and it is the most important one on the list, because it reorganizes everything above it.

The title is not a property of the video.

I want you to sit with that, because it sounds obvious once it’s said and almost nobody behaves as if it’s true.

We all treat a headline as though it’s a description of what’s underneath it, the way a label on a can describes the soup. Slightly inflated, maybe. Dressed up. But connected.

It isn’t connected. It’s a separate object, attached afterward, and it can be detached and replaced while the thing underneath it does not change at all.

Which means judging a video by its title is a category error, and here’s the part that costs me something: it’s a category error in both directions.

I have spent a week telling you that a bad title should make you suspicious. And that’s still useful as a signal about the publisher. But today I have to add the other half, and the other half is the one I got wrong.

A good title is not evidence of a good video either.

Because yesterday I looked at an accurate title and I said, out loud, on this channel, that the title was basically true and that this was news. And I was right about the title I was shown. I was wrong to treat it as the video’s title, because it wasn’t the video’s title, it was yesterday’s title, and today there’s a different one.

I graded a variable as if it were a constant. That’s the error, and it’s mine, and I’d rather name it precisely than apologize vaguely.

The repair is straightforward and it makes my whole framework cleaner. From now on, the title tells you about the person who published it, and only the body tells you about the content. Those are two separate evaluations of two separate things, and a change in one tells you nothing about the other.

Now, the part that I find genuinely grim, and I want to be careful not to oversell it, because I’m about to infer something.

Why would somebody re-title a video they’d already published?

There’s a boring answer and there’s an interesting answer, and the boring answer is probably at least partly true. Sometimes a title gets changed because of a typo, or because the original was unclear, or because somebody noticed a better framing.

But look at the direction of the change. The original title described the video. The new title describes something that is not in the video.

You do not accidentally edit toward a trade that does not exist.

So the likeliest explanation is the one everybody in the business already knows about, which is that titles get tested. You publish with one, you watch what happens, and if the numbers are disappointing you swap it for another one and watch again. It is routine, it is measurable, and it works.

And if that’s what happened here, then one sentence follows, and it is the bleakest thing I’ve said in two weeks.

The honest title was tried, and it lost.

Somebody published a title that accurately described a video containing an injury report, a practice report, a sequenced plan and a falsifiable prediction. The audience, collectively, did not reward it enough. So it was replaced with four words about a trade that does not exist.

I don’t think that’s a story about one producer being cynical. I think that’s a story about what gets clicked, which means it’s partly a story about us.

Which is uncomfortable, and I’m going to leave it uncomfortable rather than tidy it up, because the one thing I can actually do about it is tell you it happened.

Now let me talk about the specific word, because of all the words that could have been bolted on, they picked the strongest one available.

Confirmed.

That is the most aggressive epistemic claim a headline can make. Breaking says something is new. Shocking says something is surprising. Report says somebody said it. Confirmed says it has been verified. It is a claim about the quality of the evidence, not about the event.

And it is attached to a thing that does not exist.

So let me give you a law, and I think it holds up better than it has any right to.

The strength of the certainty word is inversely related to the strength of the evidence.

Think about how actual news reads. When something has genuinely been confirmed, the headline describes the thing. Team trades player for third-round pick. That’s it. The certainty is demonstrated by the specificity, not asserted by an adjective, because a real event comes with details and the details do the work.

Confirmed, in capital letters, with no object attached to it, is what you write when there is nothing to describe. The word is doing the job the facts would be doing if there were any.

And the practical test is as easy as every other one on this list. Read the word confirmed, and then ask: confirmed by whom, and what exactly? If the headline can’t answer either question, then confirmed is not a report about evidence. It’s a decoration on an absence.

The deadline is November tenth. That’s about two weeks away. Dallas is two and four and behind the Giants in its own division, which puts them in the most awkward possible position for a deadline, because they are neither clearly a buyer nor clearly a seller.

That’s a hypothetical. It was labeled as a hypothetical by the man who raised it. And it has now been through two different headlines on two different videos this week, one of which called it a blockbuster trade and the other of which called it confirmed.

That’s the whole chain. An analyst’s clearly-labeled suggestion becomes a blockbuster, and then it becomes confirmed, and nothing happened at any point in that process except words getting stronger.

And since we’re here, let me restate the two principles I’d apply to any deadline move, because they’re more useful than any rumor.

The first one is the one I gave you Thursday, and I think it’s the most useful trade idea of my season. You cannot trade for assignment clarity. The market sells players. It does not sell communication. If your problem is that two defenders think they have the same receiver, there is no pass rusher at any price who fixes it, because the problem isn’t on the field, it’s in the install.

And the second is the one about timing, which I’ll compress. You are never trading into a neutral market. The moment you’re comfortable enough to sell, buyers know you don’t have to, so the price is low. The moment you need to buy, sellers know you have to, so the price is high. The tilt is always against you, and the more visible your need, the steeper it gets.

A two-and-four team buying an edge rusher in November is a team paying full retail for a position of visible desperation. Which is why my honest preference is that this front office does nothing at all at the deadline, and I’d rather see nothing than see urgency.

And the most professionally disciplined line in the whole video is about how to use that list. Dallas needs to prepare for the players who could return instead of treating Philadelphia’s injury list like a list of guaranteed absences.

That is the correct way to read an injury report, and it generalizes. An injury report is a probability distribution, not a roster. Every name on it has some chance of playing, and a game plan built on an opponent’s absences is a game plan with no fallback.

Then the offensive plan, which is where this video argued with me and won, and I’ll restate the resolution because it’s the best thinking I did this week and it wasn’t mine alone.

The analyst says to make the running game useful, not to prove a point but to create shorter third downs and stop the defense from expecting a pass on every snap. And he says five easy yards can help this offense more than another difficult throw downfield.

On Friday I’d argued the opposite. I had Dak Prescott’s yards per completion at just over ten, a career low, and I said the problem was that the completions weren’t traveling and my top watch item was completions that travel.

My error was treating an average like a target. There are two ways to produce ten yards per completion. Everything is ten yards, which is a flat distribution and the worst offense you can build. Or most completions are four and five yards with two or three a game going for forty, which is bimodal and is how every good passing offense in the league actually works.

So he and I were describing the same healthy distribution from opposite ends, and the thing we were both against, without either of us saying it clearly, was the middle. The contested fifteen-yard throw is the hardest one to complete, the most interception-prone, and exactly what a two-high safety defense is built to take away.

And he was right about order, which is the part I got wrong. You can’t start with the deep ball. The short game and the run are what force a safety to care about what’s in front of him, and a safety’s attention moving forward is the only thing that ever creates a deep ball. The short game isn’t the opposite of the deep shot. It’s the price of it.

The one line in there that unlocked everything for me was about down and distance, and the analyst says it in passing without developing it. A five-yard completion on first down is progress. A five-yard completion on third and eight is a punt. Identical throw, identical execution, and the only difference is the situation.

Which gave me the first mechanical explanation I’ve had for Dallas’s third-down problem. What if the distribution is flat across downs? Same ten-yard menu on first down and on third and eight. That produces exactly five of thirteen on third down, and it’s precisely what an offense whose own coordinator says it’s still finding an identity would look like, because an identity is a different answer for each situation and this offense may only have one answer.

Then the defensive plan, where the best piece of coaching language in the video lives.

Cover the sticks.

On third and seven, the line to gain is seven yards away. If your underneath zone defenders settle at five yards, you have built a defense where every completion in front of you is a first down. The coverage worked, the receiver was blanketed, and the sticks moved anyway. Covering the sticks means your depth is set by the distance rather than by habit. On third and seven you settle at eight.

That is the mechanism behind the pattern I’ve been describing all month, which the analyst states perfectly: winning two plays means nothing if the opponent converts the next one. And it is not an effort problem or a talent problem. It’s depth discipline, which is a week of walkthroughs, and genuinely one of the few things wrong with this team that could be fixed in seven days.

And on the pass rush, the line I’d underline. Sending more defenders isn’t useful if the coverage leaves an easy completion behind them.

Which connects to the thing I worked out on Friday and want to repeat one more time before kickoff, because I think it’s going to matter tomorrow night.

Dak Prescott has the best sack-avoidance rate in the league, at two point seven seven percent. And this analyst says the offense struggles with pressure. Those look contradictory and they’re both true, because a sack is not the only price of pressure, it’s just the only one that shows up in a box score.

When an extra rusher comes and the ball is out in one point eight seconds to a receiver four yards downfield, nothing bad appears in any statistic. No sack, no hit, a completion. And what actually happened is that the offense paid for the pressure in yards instead of sacks. The progression got truncated. The called play did not happen and a cheaper one happened instead.

So if Fangio brings extra rushers tomorrow night, Dak probably won’t get sacked, and the ball will come out five yards downfield on third and eight, and the sack column will be empty while Dallas punts. If you judge the protection by the sack total, you will think it held.
