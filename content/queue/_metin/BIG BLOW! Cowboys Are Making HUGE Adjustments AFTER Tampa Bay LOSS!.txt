Now, Luke Schoonmaker as an H-back, and let me explain what that actually means because the term gets thrown around without definition.

A traditional tight end lines up on the line of scrimmage, attached to the tackle. An H-back lines up off the line, in the backfield or just behind it, as a hybrid between a tight end and a fullback.

And the reason that matters is one word. Motion.

A player on the line of scrimmage is mostly stuck. A player off the line can move before the snap, which means he can show the defense one strength and then attack the other, change blocking angles, lead through a hole, or chip a rusher on his way into a route.

And here’s the strategic prize, which the source identifies correctly. The same personnel group can show the defense different looks without changing who’s on the field.

That is formation manipulation, and it’s the thing I explained after the Houston game when Dallas put CeeDee Lamb and George Pickens on the same side to decide who covered whom.

Why does it matter so much? Because defenses substitute based on personnel. They see two tight ends come out and they send in a heavier package. But if the same eleven players can line up as a run formation or a spread formation, the defense has to defend both with whatever it already has on the field.

You’re not beating them with better players. You’re beating them with information they can’t get in time.

And it connects straight back to what I told you three days ago. I said Tampa Bay blitzed on thirty-five percent of their pass rush snaps last season, third highest in the league, and that tight end motion and chips were the real tactical answer because a blitz is a timed, coordinated thing and late motion ruins the timing.

I put that on my watch list and said I needed the tape. I still don’t have the snap-by-snap, so that item stays open. But given that Dallas gave up two turnovers on footballs that hit receivers’ hands, protection wasn’t the headline failure that night.

Look at everything is the cheapest sentence in coaching.

Every coach in every sport says it after every loss. It commits to nothing, it costs nothing, and it buys a week.

Yesterday I gave you a rule for telling development from stagnation. Development has a direction. Stagnation has an explanation. A developing unit shows you a trend and doesn’t need to narrate it. A stagnating one produces a new reason every week.

Look at everything is an explanation-shaped sentence until something specific changes.

So if everything really is being evaluated, the cheapest available change is the one you’d expect to see first. If nothing moves in the place where moving is free, then nothing is being evaluated anywhere.

One last thing, because the source raises a tension and I think the resolution is sharper than it realized.

Schottenheimer said they believe in what they’re doing, and also that they’ll evaluate everything. The source says both can be true, and it’s right.

But here’s the precise version. Those aren’t just compatible. They’re the only sane combination.

Scheme conviction plus personnel flexibility is how good teams operate. You hold your principles and you change the people executing them, because five games is not enough to abandon a system and is plenty to notice a role that isn’t working.

And the failure mode is the exact inverse. Scheme flexibility plus personnel loyalty. A staff that keeps tinkering with the system while refusing to change who plays. That’s the version that kills seasons, because it creates a unit that’s always learning something new and never being held accountable.

So that’s the thing to watch. Not whether they change. Which direction they change in.
