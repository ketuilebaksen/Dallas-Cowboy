Alright. Now the football, because the numbers in here deserve a proper treatment.

First, let me tell you what passer rating when targeted actually is, because it’s quoted constantly and understood rarely.

It’s the quarterback’s rating on throws aimed at a particular defender. It combines completion percentage, yards per attempt, touchdowns per attempt and interceptions per attempt into one figure. So it isn’t a measure of the corner directly. It’s a measure of how well quarterbacks do when they try him.

Sixty point eight is elite. A quarterback posting sixty point eight is having a bad afternoon. A hundred and fourteen point five is a quarterback having a very good one, every single week.

So the swing from sixty point eight to a hundred and fourteen point five is fifty-three point seven points of passer rating, and that is not a decline. That is a different player.

And then the ranking. Ninety-second of a hundred and two is the bottom tenth of the position. Ten men worse than him in a league where every team starts at least three corners.

Now the credit where it’s due, because the video handles this with more care than most. It says the statistics do not tell every detail of every play. It says the ranking does not erase his previous accomplishments and is not a complete account of his performance on every snap. Both of those are correct and both are the sort of sentence that usually gets left out.

Now the 2023 season, because this is where I think almost everybody has reasoned badly, including Dallas.

In his All-Pro year, Bland intercepted nine passes and returned five of them for touchdowns.

Five. Pick-sixes. In one season.

Now I want you to separate those two numbers, because they are not the same kind of achievement and Dallas paid as though they were.

Nine interceptions is a real skill signal. Ball production from a cornerback is one of the more repeatable things at the position, because it comes from recognition, from breaking on the throw and from catching a football, and men who do that once tend to do it again. Not nine times again, because nine is enormous. But the underlying ability is real.

Five interception returns for touchdowns is a different animal entirely.

Think about what has to happen. You have to intercept the ball, and you have to intercept it while moving forward, and you have to do it in a part of the field with space ahead of you, and the eleven men who were just blocking for a pass have to be badly positioned to tackle you, and nobody can catch you.

Almost all of that is circumstance. The catch is yours. The seventy yards afterwards are substantially a gift of geometry.

So here’s the thing I’d want the people who negotiate contracts to think about.

The interceptions earned him a contract. The touchdowns set the price.

And five pick-sixes in a season is the most volatile event in football occurring five times in sixteen games to one man. That is not a rate. That is the most extreme possible version of the distribution problem I have been hammering on all month, and it happened inside the sample that established his value.

Which means Dallas paid for a season that was partly luck and has spent three years disappointed that the luck didn’t repeat.

And I want to be careful, because that is not a criticism of the player. He didn’t set his own price. He caught nine footballs and ran five of them back, which is a magnificent season and a thing to be proud of. The error, if there is one, belongs to whoever treated it as a baseline.

And since I’ve spent a page on how the contract happened, let me do the thing nobody doing this story does, which is ask what Dallas can actually do about it now. Because there are only three options and two of them aren’t available.

Option one, trade him. That’s the one that fits the week, with a deadline on Tuesday. And it’s close to impossible, because nobody trades for a cornerback ranked ninety-second of a hundred and two who is owed the remainder of a ninety-two million dollar deal. A contract is part of the asset. An expensive underperforming contract is a liability with a man attached.

Option two, release him. And this is where the mechanics matter, so let me give them to you, because almost nobody understands why bad contracts don’t simply vanish.

When a team signs a player, the signing bonus is paid up front in cash and then spread across the years of the deal for salary cap purposes. That spreading is called proration. If you release the player before the deal ends, every remaining prorated chunk accelerates and hits your cap immediately. That’s dead money. You pay for a player you no longer have, and you pay for him faster than if you’d kept him.

Which produces the rule. Cutting a player does not save you the money you’ve already promised him. It changes when you pay it and takes the player away.

So releasing a man two years into a four-year ninety-two million dollar contract is usually the most expensive possible way to be rid of him, and in November, mid-season, with a roster already thin at the position, it is not a real option.

Option three, which is the only live one. Play him less, or play him somewhere else, and wait.

And that’s what makes Parker’s comment interesting rather than just blunt. He is talking about a player the organisation cannot move and cannot afford to discard, which means the only available route is the one he described. Finishing plays. Getting back to consistency. Coaching the man you have.

So when you hear anybody suggest Dallas should do something about DaRon Bland this week, the honest answer is that there is nothing to do except what the coordinator just said out loud.

And I’d add the thing I have to keep saying because it’s the trap that catches everybody. You can think the contract was a mistake and still think releasing him would be a worse one. Those are not contradictory positions. A sunk cost is a reason to ignore the money you’ve spent when deciding what to do next, and it is not a reason to spend more money to feel better about it.

Now the injury, because it’s the thing that actually explains the numbers and because there’s an observation in here that nobody has made.

Bland suffered a foot injury, it has affected him across multiple seasons rather than clearing up, and he had a second surgery in January.

Let me put him on the four gates, which I built weeks ago. Is he closer, is he cleared, is he available, is he effective.

Bland is at gate four. He is cleared, he is available, he is playing every week. He is not effective. And gate four is the one nobody tracks, because gates one through three produce injury reports and gate four produces a passer rating.

Which is why cleared is a binary and ready is not, and why a man can be entirely healthy by a doctor’s definition and not be the player he was.

And now the mechanism, because a foot injury for a cornerback is not a generic ailment.

A cornerback’s entire job happens in his feet. The backpedal, the transition where he flips his hips and turns to run, the plant that redirects him when a receiver breaks. All of it is force through the foot, repeated, at full speed, with a man in front of him trying to make him do it twice in one second.

So a foot that won’t take a hard plant doesn’t make a corner slower in a straight line. It makes him late out of every break. He arrives.

And now read Parker’s description of him again, because this is the thing I want you to notice.

Parker said the frustrating thing for him and for us has been he’s been close and contesting, and that now it’s about the final piece of just finishing those plays.

Close and contesting.

That is precisely what a man with a compromised change of direction looks like. He recognises the route, he breaks on it, he gets there, and he gets there a tenth of a second after he used to, which is the difference between a pass defended and a catch allowed.

So the coach’s football description and the medical history are describing the same thing from two directions, and the coach never mentions the injury once.

I don’t think that’s evasion. I think it’s a coach describing what he sees on film and declining to diagnose a player in public, which is the correct thing for a coach to do.
