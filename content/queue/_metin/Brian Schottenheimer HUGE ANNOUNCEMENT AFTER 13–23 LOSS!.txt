Now, I want to do the coverage argument properly, because the source makes a genuinely sophisticated point and then makes an even better one, and the second one is the part that matters.

The case for cover three against Jordan Love is specific. He has struggled against it this season, with more of his interceptions and sacks coming against that coverage. And cover three is already something Christian Parker calls often, which we know independently, because Schottenheimer told us this defense has played its highest cover three rate of the season as part of the simplification.

So the call fits the opponent and fits what Dallas already knows. That’s a good plan.

But then the source says the thing that elevates it, and I want to read you the idea because it’s exactly right.

Opponents have completed passes at a high rate against Dallas’s cover three looks, and there haven’t been enough takeaways. So simply calling the right coverage isn’t enough, and Parker should use it strategically rather than treating it as a magic solution.

That is the single smartest sentence I’ve been handed in two weeks, and let me explain why, because it generalizes.

A coverage is a shape, not an outcome.

Cover three describes where eleven men stand and who is responsible for which area. It does not describe how well any of them play. Two teams can call the identical coverage on the identical down and get opposite results, because the coverage doesn’t cover anybody. People do.

And here’s the specific failure mode, which explains Dallas’s whole season.

Cover three puts three defenders deep and four underneath. The three deep take away the shots over the top, which is exactly what you want against a team that lives on explosive plays.

The four underneath have a different job. They have to rally to the football and tackle in space.

So a team playing cover three correctly gives up short completions by design and takes them away by execution. The completion happens. The yards after it shouldn’t.

Now look at Dallas’s season. Over seventy percent completions allowed in every single game. Poor tackling. Missed assignments. That’s not a coverage failure. That’s the underneath half of a correct coverage not closing.

Which means the fix was never a different call. The fix is rallying and tackling, and that is the most coachable thing in football, and it’s exactly what you’d work on during ten extra days.

Alright. Now Mike Pettine, and this is the detail that answers a question I asked eight days ago.

When the hire was announced, I raised what I called the two cooks problem. I said there’s a specific failure mode when you add a senior voice above a young coordinator mid-season, because on game day only one person can call a defense. Eleven men, forty seconds between snaps, one green dot. That channel cannot be shared.

And my suggested solution was to separate them in time. Pettine in the meeting rooms Monday through Thursday, where Parker is weakest. Parker alone on the headset Sunday, where he’s strongest.

Here’s what Dallas actually did.

Christian Parker stays down on the sideline. Mike Pettine works from the booth.

And I want to say plainly that their answer is better than mine.

Here’s why. I was separating them by day. They separated them by function, which keeps Pettine useful on Sunday instead of sending him home on Friday.

And the division is exactly right, because the booth and the sideline are good at completely different things.

From the booth you can see all twenty-two players at once. You can watch a formation develop from above, see the safety rotation before the snap, identify the offensive line’s slide, and recognize a tendency across a series. You cannot do any of that from field level, where you’re looking through bodies at a flat angle.

From the sideline you can do the one thing the booth cannot. You can look a man in the eye between series. You can grab a linebacker and say the thing he needs to hear in eight seconds. You can feel whether a group is rattled.

So you put the structural eyes upstairs and the human voice on the grass.

And crucially, this does not create two cooks, and here’s the mechanism. The booth coach feeds information down. The field coach makes the call. Information flows one direction and authority stays in one place.

That’s not a compromise. That’s how good staffs have always worked, and I should have thought of it. I was solving the right problem with a worse tool.

The first thing. A man taking the blame for something that isn’t entirely his fault is doing the single most valuable thing a leader can do in a losing locker room.

Because here’s the mechanism. You cannot have a room where everybody is honest about their own failures if the best player is explaining why his are somebody else’s. Accountability only travels downward from the top of a depth chart. If your most accomplished defensive lineman stands up and says I need to beat more double teams, then nobody else in that room can say the scheme is the problem, and suddenly eleven men are each fixing their own piece.

The second thing, and I’d be dishonest to skip it. A player taking the blame can let an organization off a hook it belongs on.

If the real issue is that a two-gap front is being asked of personnel built to penetrate, then no amount of individual effort fixes it, and a leader absorbing that blame can delay the structural conversation.

The structure is the bigger cause. And he can still beat more double teams than he’s beating. Those are both facts, and the professional attitude is to work on the one you control, which is exactly what he said.
