And here’s the precise reason, which is a football reason rather than a procedural one.

Three hundred and three yards eliminates the explanations that had nothing to do with the play-caller. It isn’t possession count, which is a defense problem. It isn’t an inability to move the ball, which is a personnel problem.

What’s left is the red zone and situational football. And those are the single most play-caller-dependent phases of an NFL game.

Think about why. Between the twenties, a lot of what happens is talent and matchups. The field is wide, there’s room behind the defense, and a good receiver beating a corner is a good receiver beating a corner.

Inside the twenty, the field shrinks. There’s no deep third anymore because the end line does that work for the defense. Every throwing window gets smaller, every defender gets closer, and the margin for error on a route combination goes to almost nothing.

Which means red zone offense is design. It’s spacing, it’s rub concepts, it’s knowing which coverage you’re about to see and having a plan that beats it. That is the play-caller’s department in a way that a twelve-yard completion on second and eight simply isn’t.

So the diagnosis has moved the problem into the one room where the call sheet lives. I’m still waiting for Monday, and I’m waiting with less confidence than I had on Saturday.

Now let me give you the detail from today’s material that I think is genuinely the best new idea I’ve had handed to me in weeks, and it’s in one sentence.

The source says Dak Prescott has been unable to connect with some of his key targets, and it specifically names George Pickens.

And I want to build on that, because I think it might be the whole thing.

Regular viewers know I’ve made an argument about Pickens all season that nobody else makes. I’ve said his value isn’t the footballs he catches. It’s the defenders he removes from everybody else’s area.

And I had the proof. Houston built their entire defensive plan around not getting beaten deep by George Pickens, and CeeDee Lamb caught seventeen passes. That’s not a coincidence, that’s cause and effect. One man draws the safety, the other man gets a one-on-one.

I called it gravity.

Now watch what happens if the gravity switches off.

If Dak and Pickens aren’t connecting, the first thing you lose is obvious. The deep completions.

But the second thing you lose is the one that matters more, and it arrives about a week later, because defensive coaches watch film.

Once a defense sees two games of a deep threat not being hit, they stop respecting it. And the moment they stop respecting it, the safety who was playing twenty yards deep over the top comes down to fifteen. Then to twelve. Then he’s in the box.

And now CeeDee Lamb has no one-on-ones. And the intermediate windows close. And your tight end, who I showed you is averaging six point eight yards a catch, has even less room. And suddenly a normal offense cannot finish a drive, because the field has shrunk from the inside out.

That’s a cascade. And here’s why I find it so persuasive.

It explains the shape of the collapse, and nothing else does.

Thirty-four points a game for three weeks, then sixteen, then thirteen. That’s a cliff, not a slope. And injuries produce slopes. A quad gets a bit worse, a thumb heals a bit slower, production drifts down.

Defensive adjustment produces cliffs. Because it happens all at once, in a meeting room, on a Tuesday, and then every opponent after that does the same thing because they all watch the same film.

So if you asked me today what single thing I’d point at, it’s that. The deep ball stopped connecting, the respect evaporated, the safeties came down, and an offense that lived on space ran out of it.

And that gives me a much better watch item for Monday night than any I’ve offered, so let me put it on the record.

Forget Pickens’s target total. Forget his catch total. I want to see one completed deep ball to George Pickens.

One. Over twenty yards, in the air, with a safety in the picture.

Because that single play buys back everything else. It pushes a safety back fifteen yards for the rest of the night, it reopens the intermediate middle, and it hands CeeDee Lamb his one-on-one back.

That’s the cheapest fix available to this offense and it doesn’t require a new play-caller, a trade, or a healthy anything. It requires one throw that arrives.
