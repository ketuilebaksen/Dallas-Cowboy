And I want to be careful about what I’m not saying. I am not saying Klayton Adams is bad at his job, and I’m not saying he said anything he shouldn’t have. He said something honest, which is rare, and the honest thing is alarming on its own without me adding to it.

But let me tell you what an identity actually is, because the word gets used as if it means a vibe, and it doesn’t. It’s a very concrete thing.

An offensive identity is the answer to four questions.

What do we run when we absolutely have to have four yards?

What do we run on third and six, every week, when the defense knows it’s coming?

What’s our two-minute staple, the thing we go to when the clock is the enemy?

And what’s our answer when the opponent takes away our best thing?

That’s it. Those four. And notice that none of them are about talent. They are about having a default, which is something you can only build by repetition, and which exists specifically for the moments when nothing else is working.

An offense without an identity is fine when things are going well, because talent covers the gap. It falls apart the moment things go badly, because it has no default to fall back on.

And now look at where this offense has actually failed.

Zero points in the first half at Lambeau Field. Five of thirteen on third down. Fourteen and a half points a game across the last two. Twenty-nine total points in two games. Manageable situations wasted on the downs that mattered.

Every one of those is a failure in exactly the situation a default is for.

And here’s the part that ties it to something I’ve been saying for a week, and I think it’s the real payoff.

I’ve been calling it an organizational signature. Three times this season, both units have been worse in the first half than in the second. And I’ve argued that an opening plan is a teachable thing while in-game adjustment mostly isn’t, so a team that is consistently bad early and better late has a planning problem rather than a coaching-in-the-moment problem.

An opening plan is an identity written down.

The first fifteen plays of a football game are the most pre-meditated snaps in the sport. Nobody is reacting. Nobody has seen anything yet. You are running the thing you decided on Wednesday that you are. And if the honest answer to what you are is still finding an identity, then those fifteen plays have nothing to be.

That’s why the first halves are empty. Not because the players are worse before halftime. Because the plan is a statement about who you are, and this offense doesn’t have one yet, so the second half, when you can finally react to what you’ve seen, is the first half in which they actually know what to do.

I’ve been describing that pattern for a week. Today the offensive coordinator explained it, and I don’t think he realized he was doing it.

So let me do the actual football, because preparing for a backup quarterback is not the simple thing people assume.

Here’s what changes structurally.

Jalen Hurts is a quarterback who runs. Not scrambles. Runs, by design, as a called part of the offense. And a defense facing a quarterback like that has to spend resources on it. Specifically, your edge players have containment responsibilities, which I’ve explained before. Contain means you rush up the field and then turn inward at the quarterback’s depth rather than running past him, because running past a mobile quarterback is how a sack becomes a twelve-yard scramble.

Containment is expensive. It slows your rush. It costs you the inside move, which is often your best move. And it has to be honored on every single snap, because the one time you don’t, that’s the play.

If the quarterback is a pocket passer, some of that bill goes away. You can rush inside. You can let your edge players use their full arsenal instead of two-thirds of it. That is a real and significant change in what Dallas’s pass rush is allowed to do, and it arrives in the same week that Mike Pettine joined the staff in the booth.

Now the honest counterweight, and the source says it well, so I’ll say it too.

Do not treat this as an easy win.

A backup quarterback is not automatically a downgrade for one game, and there are three reasons.

First, you have less tape on him, and so does everybody else. Defensive game plans are built on tendencies, and a man with few starts doesn’t have many. You’re preparing for a projection.

Second, an offense can simplify around a backup. Fewer reads, more defined throws, more help. A simplified offense is often more efficient for one game, not less, because the things it does badly have been removed from the menu.

Third, and this is the one that actually decides games, the rest of that offense is unchanged. The source names them. DeVonta Smith is a dangerous receiver regardless of who’s throwing. Saquon Barkley’s availability matters. The offensive line is the same line.

So the correct frame is not Philadelphia is weaker. It’s Philadelphia is different, and the plan Dallas built on Wednesday for one quarterback has to work against another one.

Here’s what a Fangio defense does. It plays with two safeties deep. It keeps the box light, meaning fewer defenders near the line of scrimmage than a traditional front. It does not blitz much, because the whole design is built on rushing four and playing sound coverage behind it. And its central principle is bend but don’t break. It will concede yardage all day and refuse to concede the thing that actually scores, which is the explosive play over the top.

It invites you to run the football.

Take the run, take four yards, take the clock, and in exchange I am going to make sure nothing goes seventy yards over my head. Alright. Now the two changes this video actually proposes, because they’re both good ideas and both deserve real analysis.

Change one is in the secondary, and it’s about DaRon Bland.

The source says he hasn’t seen enough to conclude the outside job automatically stays Bland’s. He grants that Bland looked better against Green Bay and calls that encouraging, but says he wants consistent coverage rather than one improved game. And he suggests that with the cornerback whose name I still cannot resolve working toward a return, Dallas could play that man outside opposite Joey Porter Junior, and move Bland inside, where Bland has experience.

Now, I want to defend this idea properly, because it will sound like a demotion and it is not one.

Moving a cornerback from the boundary to the nickel is not a punishment. In a modern defense it is arguably the harder job and it is certainly the busier one.

Here’s why. The boundary corner has the sideline as a teammate. The sideline is an extra defender that never misses a tackle, because a receiver who runs out of bounds is covered. The nickel defender has no sideline. He has grass on both sides of him, he is the man covering the slot receiver who gets a free release and a two-way go, he is frequently the designated blitzer, and he has run-fit responsibilities on every single snap because he’s inside the formation.

More snaps, more responsibilities, no sideline.

And there’s a second reason it’s a real idea rather than a shuffle. A ball-hawking cornerback is a player whose value depends on how many footballs come near him. And how many footballs come near you is overwhelmingly a function of where you line up, not how good you are. I’ve made this point about targets before. There are three reasons a cornerback isn’t thrown at, and only one of them is that he’s good. The other two are that he’s playing a side quarterbacks avoid, and that his help structure makes the throw unappealing.

The slot sees more football than the boundary. That’s not an opinion, it’s where offenses live. So moving a man with ball skills closer to where the ball goes is a deliberate attempt to convert a talent into production.

And then the source says something I want to highlight, because it’s the sharpest sentence in the video.

A player’s reputation shouldn’t prevent the coaches from adjusting his role when the defense needs something different.

That is the sunk-cost argument, applied to playing time instead of draft picks.

I made the draft-pick version on Tuesday. You do not keep a player on the roster because you drafted him, because the pick is already spent and keeping him for that reason means paying twice for the same decision.

Reputation works identically. A reputation is a record of what a man did in the past. It has already been earned, it cannot be unearned, and it is not a claim on future snaps. Alignment is a tool, not a reward, and a coach who won’t move a good player because moving him looks like a demotion is managing optics instead of a defense.

Now, the one place I want to add a constraint, because the source asks for something that isn’t free.

He also says he’d like the safety whose name I can’t place playing deeper more often.

And I understand the instinct completely. A safety who’s deeper takes away the thing that actually beats you. But here’s the arithmetic, and it’s fixed-sum.

You have eleven men. The box is the area near the line of scrimmage, and every defender you move out of it to play deeper is a defender who is no longer there against the run. That’s not a philosophy, it’s subtraction.

So if a safety has been playing shallow, there’s usually a reason, and the reason is almost always that the run defense needs him. Dallas has allowed eight hundred and ninety-nine rushing yards in six games. That number is what’s pulling safeties down toward the line of scrimmage in the first place.

Which means the request to play him deeper is a request to accept more rushing yards. That may well be the right trade, especially against a passing offense. But it is a trade, and any analysis that asks for better coverage and better run defense out of the same eleven men is asking for something that does not exist.

That’s the honest version, and I’d rather hand you the constraint than the wish.

Now, the piece of new information in this section that I did not have.

The source refers to Jaishawn Barham as a rookie linebacker.

I’ve been describing him as a young developing player. Rookie is more specific and it changes the frame, so let me use it properly, because the critique attached to it is the most common and most correct thing anybody says about a first-year linebacker.

The source says he likes the aggression but wants it under control, and that for a rookie linebacker, knowing when to attack and when to stay patient is a huge part of the job.

That’s the whole position in one sentence, and here’s the mechanism.

A linebacker’s first job is to read a run key, usually a guard or a tackle, and go. The faster you go, the better you are against a straight-ahead run. That’s the skill that shows up immediately, and it’s what everybody means when they say a young linebacker plays fast and physical. Barham attacks downhill and gets involved near the line of scrimmage, which is exactly that skill.

But offenses are built to punish exactly that instinct. Counter, where the first key lies to you. Play-action, which doesn’t fool anybody but does buy one hard step in the wrong direction. The cutback, where the back lets your speed carry you out of the play. Every one of those is designed specifically against a linebacker who trusts his first read completely.

So patience is not the absence of aggression. Patience is a second step that confirms the first one. And it arrives late in a career because it requires having been burned, which means the only way to get it is to play badly first, in games, where it costs you.

Which is why a defense wants the option to spread those snaps around, and why Overshown returning does not take Barham’s job. It gives Parker the ability to put a rookie in the situations he’s already good at instead of all of them.
