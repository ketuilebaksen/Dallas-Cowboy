Let’s do my own audit first, because I’d rather grade myself than have the comments do it.

Three days ago I was handed a video arguing Dallas should trade for Bucky Irving, and I said no. Let me tell you precisely what I said, because the details matter.

I said, and I’m quoting myself, he’s a genuinely productive young back, his rookie season went past a thousand rushing yards, that’s a good football player, that’s not in dispute.

Then I gave three reasons for saying no.

Reason one. He plays for Tampa Bay, the team Dallas was in the middle of playing that week.

Reason two. He’s on a rookie contract through 2027, which makes him the most expensive asset class in football, and Tampa Bay has no reason on earth to answer the phone.

Reason three. Dallas doesn’t need him, because Dallas leads the National Football League in points per drive.

Now hold a hundred sixty-five yards up against each of those.

Reason one got confirmed in the most humiliating way available. I said you don’t trade for the opposing team’s starting running back the week you play them, and then he ran for a hundred sixty-five yards against you. If anything, I understated it. That wasn’t a scheduling awkwardness. That was a man on the other sideline with a football.

Reason two got stronger, not weaker. A back who just ran for a hundred sixty-five yards in a prime time win is more expensive today than he was last week, not less. Tampa Bay has even less reason to sell. The price went up.

Reason three I still believe, and I’ll defend it. Dallas did not lose that game because of its running back. Javonte Williams scored three rushing touchdowns against Houston and has been productive all season. The problem was never the Dallas backfield.

So all three reasons hold. Technically, I was right.

And yet I want to be honest about what I got wrong, because something in that video deserves a correction and it isn’t the conclusion.

It’s the tone.

I treated that proposal as absurd. I built a whole segment around the video burying its own player’s name for nine minutes, and I was correct about that, and I let the bad sourcing bleed into how seriously I took the player. A hundred sixty-five yards says the player evaluation in that garbage video was, if anything, too modest.

So here’s the lesson, and it’s a refinement of the rule I gave you last week. I told you sourcing quality and idea quality are separate axes. Today I’d add a third axis. Player quality is separate from both.

A video can be worthless, its trade idea can be unworkable, and its player can still be excellent. All three of those can be true at once, and I collapsed the third into the first two because it was rhetorically convenient.

Now, the part that makes me feel better about my own trade board, because there’s a genuine payoff here.

Two days after the Irving video, I endorsed trading a fourth-round pick for Khalil Mack. And my specific argument was that Dallas does not need a better pass rusher, it needs a pass rusher it can trust on first down. I said Dallas doesn’t need another situational speed guy to stand next to Von Miller, it needs somebody who can be on the field on first and ten and set an edge in the run game.

A hundred sixty-five yards from a running back is the loudest possible endorsement of that recommendation.

So the two halves of my own trade board collided in the most literal way imaginable. The player I declined to buy ran for a hundred sixty-five yards against the exact weakness I said to spend money on.

I’ll take that trade. Say no to the back, say yes to the man who tackles him.

Now here’s why the Mayfield absence is the most damning detail of the season, and this is the argument I most want you to take away today.

Tampa Bay ran the football thirty-nine times for two hundred forty-one yards. More than six yards per carry.

Thirty-nine carries.

Now ask yourself why a team runs it thirty-nine times.

Not because they’re a great rushing team by identity. Because their starting quarterback was unavailable and they were protecting an undrafted rookie in his second career start.

Which means Tampa Bay announced their game plan a week in advance.

Every single defensive coordinator in professional football knows what’s coming when the other team’s quarterback is out and a second-start rookie is in. You know. Everybody knows. The opponent is going to hand the football off, stay on schedule, keep it simple, and ask the rookie to do as little as possible.

There was no mystery. There was no disguise. There was nothing to figure out.

And Dallas gave up two hundred forty-one yards on thirty-nine carries anyway.

That’s what I mean by damning rather than alarming. An alarming number is one you didn’t see coming. A damning number is one you saw coming, prepared for, and still surrendered.

And it rules something out. This is not a preparation failure. The staff cannot have been surprised. Which means the breakdown is technique and structure, and that lands exactly on the diagnosis I gave you yesterday about two-gap responsibility in a three-four front.

If you know they’re running, and you’ve prepared to stop the run, and you still give up six yards a carry, then the men up front are losing their gaps. Full stop.

One more number from that game that I didn’t have. Dallas committed three turnovers and recorded zero takeaways.

Zero.

That’s a turnover margin of minus three in a single football game, which is close to unwinnable regardless of anything else. And it makes DaRon Bland’s dropped interception sting more than I realized, because that was the one takeaway on the table all night.

Alright. Mike Pettine, and I have real updating to do.

First, the full résumé, which is bigger than what I had yesterday.

He has been a defensive coordinator for the New York Jets, the Buffalo Bills, and the Green Bay Packers. He spent two seasons as head coach of the Cleveland Browns. And more recently he worked with the Minnesota Vikings as assistant head coach and outside linebackers coach.

Three coordinator jobs, a head coaching job, and an assistant head coach job. And note that last title, because outside linebackers coach is a three-four job. In a three-four, the outside linebackers are the edge setters and the edge rushers. That’s not a footnote on a résumé, that’s the exact position group whose failure I described yesterday.

Now the thing I got wrong.

Yesterday I raised what I called the honest risk of this hire. I said there’s a specific failure mode when you add a senior voice above a young coordinator mid-season, because on game day only one person can call a defense. I called it the two cooks problem and I said the right implementation is Pettine in the meeting rooms Monday through Thursday and Parker alone on the headset Sunday.

I stand by the structural point. But I substantially overrated the risk, and here’s why.

Mike Pettine was Green Bay’s defensive coordinator from 2018 through 2020. And Christian Parker worked on that staff, as a quality control coach, during Pettine’s final two seasons.

Parker worked under Pettine.

That changes everything about how this reads.

This is not a stranger being imposed on a struggling young coordinator by a nervous front office. This is a man who was Christian Parker’s boss, arriving to help Christian Parker, at a point in Parker’s career when Parker is doing the job Pettine used to do.

And think about what quality control actually means as a job, because it’s not a vague title. The quality control coach is the person who breaks down film, tracks opponent tendencies, catalogues self-scouting data, and prepares the material the coordinator uses to build a plan.

So Christian Parker spent two years preparing film for Mike Pettine.

Which means the deference question, the one that makes these arrangements awkward, was settled six years ago. They already know how to work together. Parker already knows how this man thinks, what he asks for, and what he’s looking at. And Pettine already knows what Parker is good at.

There is no learning curve in the relationship. The only learning curve is the football.

So I’d downgrade my concern from real to low. Yesterday I said I’d bet on them getting it right. Today I’d say the structure was already built.

And the source I was handed today handles this exactly correctly, which I want to credit, because it’s rare. It says Parker remains the defensive coordinator, Pettine was announced as a senior defensive assistant rather than a replacement, and then it says, and I love this line, let’s not turn this into a coaching power struggle without evidence.

That’s a source refusing to manufacture drama. After the month I’ve had, reading that was genuinely refreshing.

Now. The two names. This is the part that explains everything.

Schottenheimer specifically referenced Pettine’s familiarity with the defensive system and with players such as Kenny Clark and Rashan Gary.

Kenny Clark and Rashan Gary.

Both of them are former Green Bay Packers. Both of them played for Mike Pettine in Green Bay.

Now put that together with what I told you yesterday, and watch the whole picture assemble.

Kenny Clark is a nose tackle. In a three-four front, the nose tackle is the two-gap anchor. He lines up over the center, he takes on the man in front of him, and he is responsible for the gaps on both sides. He is the single most important and most thankless job in that entire structure, because if the nose gets moved, the front collapses and the linebackers have nothing to flow to.

Rashan Gary is an outside linebacker in that front. Edge setter against the run, edge rusher against the pass, both jobs on every snap.

So Dallas did not merely install a three-four defense.

Dallas installed a three-four defense and imported the personnel from Mike Pettine’s Green Bay three-four to play the two most structurally important positions in it.

And now Dallas has imported Mike Pettine.

That is not a reactive hire. That’s the completion of a plan that started in the offseason, and I don’t think anybody has said that out loud.

And it reframes a criticism I made earlier this season, so let me own that too. I was hard on Rashan Gary after the Baltimore game, when he ran at Lamar Jackson with his hands in the air. I called it a technique for rushing a statue. Then I refined it when he had his best game as a Cowboy, and I reframed him as a pocket-quarterback weapon who’d drawn three mobile quarterbacks in a row.

Both of those readings were about Gary as a pass rusher. Neither of them considered that he was being asked to do a job in a front structure that his previous coordinator taught him, under a coordinator who learned that structure as a quality control coach.

Which means the most likely explanation for Gary’s inconsistency isn’t talent or effort. It’s that he knows what the job is supposed to look like and the install around him hasn’t matched it.

That’s testable, by the way. If Rashan Gary’s play improves noticeably in the next three weeks, the explanation isn’t that he found motivation. It’s that somebody who taught him the position arrived in the building.

Now, the Green Bay game, because the schedule has a sense of humor.

Dallas visits Green Bay on October the eighteenth. Pettine was hired on the twelfth.

So six days after Dallas hires Mike Pettine, Dallas plays the team where Pettine was the defensive coordinator and where Christian Parker worked for him.

And I want to credit the source again, because it handles this with restraint that I’d have struggled to show. It says Pettine’s previous experience doesn’t mean he automatically knows everything the current Green Bay staff will do, because coaches and players change and schemes evolve.

That is exactly right and it’s the difference between analysis and a narrative. Pettine left Green Bay after 2020. That’s a long time in football. Whatever he knows about that building is six years old.

What he can genuinely offer is institutional feel rather than a playbook. How that organization thinks, what it values, how it tends to attack. That’s real but it’s soft, and anybody promising you an insider advantage is selling.

And the source makes the more important point, which is that Dallas cannot spend the week studying Green Bay while ignoring what Tampa Bay did to them. The opponent is not the problem. The gaps are the problem.

So what’s actually achievable in six days?

Yesterday I argued Pettine can’t re-install a front in a week, because changing a lineman from two-gap to one-gap responsibility is a training camp project. You’re changing his first step, his hands and his read, and those are habits.

Today’s source reaches the same conclusion and words it better than I did. It says introducing too many changes too quickly could create confusion if players aren’t comfortable with their assignments. And it says the first improvements may need to be simple. Line up correctly, understand the assignment, tackle consistently, and stop giving away easy yards.

That’s the right answer and it’s also the whole simplify arc I’ve tracked since Week One. Simplify was the demand after nine of eleven third downs were converted. Then the head coach said the word. Then we saw it in the cover three percentage. And yesterday I argued the hardest part of simplifying isn’t cutting, it’s knowing what’s safe to cut, and that knowledge is mileage rather than intelligence.

Three coordinator jobs and a head coaching job is the mileage. That’s what they bought.

One last thing, because the source closes on the best question in the whole story and I want to answer it properly.

Is this a coaching problem, a player problem, or both?

And the source is right that the honest answer requires film nobody watching at home has. A long run can happen because a lineman got moved out of his gap, because a linebacker took a bad angle, because somebody missed a tackle, or because the opponent’s blocking scheme simply won. Sometimes all four on the same play.

But I can narrow it, using what we now know, and here’s the narrowing.

If a defender doesn’t understand his responsibility, that’s coaching. If he understands it and loses the matchup anyway, that’s personnel. And if the opponent keeps attacking the same alignment, that’s scheme.

Now apply the Mayfield fact. Tampa Bay’s quarterback was out, everyone knew they’d run, Dallas prepared to stop the run, and thirty-nine carries went for two hundred forty-one yards.

That eliminates surprise. It eliminates the opponent being clever. It does not look like a defense being out-thought.

It looks like a defense that knew exactly what was coming and could not execute the technique required to stop it.

And that is the single most hopeful reading available, which is going to sound strange. Here’s why.

An out-thought defense needs a new mind. An out-executed defense needs reps, technique, and somebody who has taught that technique before.

Dallas just hired a man who coached the nose tackle and the outside linebacker in that exact front.

That’s the right medicine for the actual illness. It may be too late and it may be too little, but it is not the wrong prescription.

Here’s what I’m watching in Green Bay.

Watch the first-down run defense specifically, because stopping the run on first down is what creates the obvious passing situations that let a pass rush exist. That’s the whole chain and it starts there.

Watch Kenny Clark at the nose, and whether he’s holding his ground or getting moved. If the man Pettine coached in this front starts anchoring it, that’s the clearest possible evidence the hire is working, and you can see it on television if you look at the middle of the line instead of the football.

Watch Rashan Gary against the run rather than for sacks, because edge setting is the job that failed and it’s the job Pettine coached.

And watch for takeaways, because zero is not a strategy and minus three in turnover margin loses to anybody.
