Now let me explain what a pre-snap communication breakdown actually is, because this phrase gets used constantly and almost never explained.

Before the snap, a defense has to answer questions. Where is the tight end lined up? Did he just move? If he releases vertically, who carries him? If the back swings out, who takes that instead?

Those answers are not automatic. They're called out loud, pointed at, and confirmed, in the seconds between the offense breaking the huddle and the ball moving.

And the tight end is the single hardest assignment in that whole conversation, because he lives in the gray area. He's too big for most defensive backs and too fast for most linebackers, and he lines up in different places specifically to create confusion about who owns him.

When an offense motions before the snap, all of those answers have to be re-established instantly. That's why motion works. It isn't magic. It's a question asked at a moment when you have no time to answer it carefully.

And remember what we covered yesterday. Pre-snap motion has been breaking this defense going back to last season. This wasn't a new problem that appeared on Sunday night. It's a problem that survived a coaching change.

That's the part that should bother you more than one linebacker's mistake.

Let me be honest about time of possession, though, because it's a stat people misuse constantly.

Time of possession does not cause you to lose. It's mostly a symptom. Teams that are converting third downs and staying on schedule naturally hold the ball longer, and teams that fall behind naturally throw more and stop the clock more. So a lopsided possession number is usually describing what already happened rather than explaining it.

But there is one place where it genuinely matters, and that's physical. Thirty-six minutes on the field is a defense that never gets to breathe. By the fourth quarter, the guys being asked to set an edge have been doing it for fifty snaps, and technique is the first thing that goes when legs go.

So it's not why Dallas lost. It's part of why the losing got worse as the night went on.

And there's a second cost to a possession number like that which nobody talks about, and it's on the other side of the ball.

When your defense is on the field for thirty-six minutes, your offense is standing on the sideline in a helmet doing nothing for thirty-six minutes.

Rhythm is a real thing in football. A quarterback who has taken twelve snaps by the middle of the third quarter is a quarterback who has not had a chance to find anything.
