First, the context. Dallas is one and one after beating Washington thirty-seven to twenty. And that win came despite a pile of injuries on defense, which honestly says something good about how this staff prepared.

After the Giants loss, Brian Schottenheimer told everybody he wasn't planning wholesale lineup changes. And after one game, that was a fair position. You don't blow up a new defense because of one bad night.

But the changes came anyway. Not by choice. By the injury report.

So let's start there, with the change nobody wanted.

Malik Hooker fractured a bone in his forearm in the Giants game and needed surgery. He missed the Washington game and he's expected to miss more time.

And I want you to understand who Dallas actually lost, because this isn't a bit-part player.

Hooker is a ten-year veteran who has started at free safety for Dallas since 2023. And here's the detail that tells you what kind of teammate he is. This offseason he came up as a potential cap casualty, and he restructured his own contract just to stay on this roster.

He wanted to be here. And the front office decided they wanted him too.

Losing that kind of steadying, experienced voice at the back of the secondary would hurt any defense. But it hurts this one more than most, because the front seven is full of young players still learning the scheme. The free safety is the last man in the chain. He's the one who sees the whole field and fixes the mistakes in front of him. When the players in front of you are young, the man behind them matters even more.

The plan all along was that if Hooker went down, Dallas would lean on P.J. Locke and Markquese Bell.

Locke was a free agent signing brought in specifically to be quality depth at safety. He played a rotational role against the Giants, and honestly, it was forgettable. To be fair to him, very few players on this defense graded out even average that night. He had company.

And then against Washington, Locke went down himself. Helped off the field by multiple staffers, barely putting weight on his leg, and later seen in a walking boot. No official diagnosis yet.

So the backup plan now needs a backup plan. Markquese Bell had to play free safety against Washington, and the depth behind him is thin.

That's the first major change to this defense, and it's the one that matters most against Baltimore. The free safety spot is in real trouble.

Now let's talk about the changes Dallas can actually choose to make, because there are three that have been argued for since the Giants game, and each one looks different now that Lamar Jackson is next.

Change number one is on the edge, and it's the most urgent.

Against the Giants, the coaching staff used rookie Malachi Lawrence as the third run-stopping edge defender. And I understand exactly why. You don't want to burn Von Miller's limited snap count setting the edge against inside zone all game. Miller is a closer. You save him for passing downs.

The problem is that Lawrence got picked on in the run game. New York hit him repeatedly with read-option looks that put him in conflict, and they won.

Let me explain what that means, because it is the single most important concept for this week.

On a read-option play, the offense deliberately leaves one defender unblocked. Usually the edge defender. The quarterback takes the snap, puts the ball in the running back's belly, and reads that unblocked man.

If the edge defender crashes down to tackle the running back, the quarterback pulls the ball and runs around him. If the edge defender stays wide to take the quarterback, the quarterback hands it off and the back runs inside him.

Whatever the defender does, the quarterback does the opposite. That's the entire design. The defense is turned into a guessing game, and the unblocked man is the one guessing.

The only answer is discipline. The edge defender has to know his exact assignment on that play, quarterback or running back, and play it without hesitating. The moment he hesitates or tries to do both, he does neither.

Against the Giants, Lawrence hesitated. And the Giants kept calling it.

Now think about who's next.

Lamar Jackson is one of the most dangerous running quarterbacks this sport has ever seen. Baltimore doesn't just run read option occasionally. It's a foundation of what they do. And they will study the Giants tape, find the edge defender who looked least sure of his job, and call that play at him until Dallas proves it can stop it.

That's how coordinators work. They don't attack your best player. They attack your most uncertain one.

So what are the options?

You could lean more on Von Miller. He's played fifteen seasons. He has seen every read-option concept a coordinator can draw up, and he knows exactly how to play it. The trade-off is his snap count. He's thirty-seven, and we've said since he signed that overusing him early is the fastest way to lose him in December.

But there's another name that I think deserves a much bigger role, and he was the bright spot of the Giants game.

Donovan Ezeiruaku. The second-year edge rusher was a legitimately good run defender against New York. If he's the one setting the edge against Baltimore, with Miller saved for obvious passing downs, that's a rotation that makes sense.

And if the veterans in front of him don't pick it up, don't be surprised if Ezeiruaku's role keeps growing all season.

Change number two is the green dot, and this one is genuinely controversial.

After the Giants game, there was a real argument that the green dot should go to Caleb Downs instead of Jaishawn Barham.

Here's that case. Barham is a true rookie learning a new position and a new defense at NFL speed. Against the Giants, he didn't look as explosive as he did in the preseason. Maybe he was physically gassed. Maybe mentally gassed. And once you're mentally worn down in this league, it starts to show up physically. There's a fair question about whether piling the green dot on top of everything else, once Overshown went down, slowed his processing.

Downs, on the other hand, was a first-round pick, and from everything reported out of camp, he processes what an offense is showing before the snap like a veteran. That's exactly what the green dot job is. Recognize the formation, get the call out on time, don't get rattled.

And here's something a lot of fans don't know. The green dot doesn't have to be a linebacker. Some teams give it to a safety.

Now the other side. The green dot is usually a linebacker for a reason. He's positioned in the middle of the formation, close to everybody he has to talk to. A safety relaying calls from deeper in the secondary has a harder job physically getting the message to eleven people. And Downs is a rookie too, still learning a new coordinator's terminology. Stacking the green dot on him is a lot to ask.

It's also fair to say Downs' tape wasn't perfect. Some have suggested he may have carried some responsibility on the Isaiah Likely touchdown before halftime, though Brian Schottenheimer publicly named Dee Winters for that play. I'm not convinced it's on Downs, but it's worth putting on the table instead of pretending his tape was flawless.

So what actually happened against Washington?

The staff gave the green dot to Barham. And then Barham got sick on the field, threw up back-to-back times, and had to come off for a stretch. Shemar James wore the green dot for a decent amount of time.

So in two games, the green dot has passed through three different players. That is not a stable situation for a defense whose biggest problem in Week One was communication.

And now there's a new wrinkle. If the safety injuries force Dallas to move Caleb Downs back to free safety for the Baltimore game, the staff has to decide whether to hand him the green dot at the same time. That would solve two problems with one player, or it would overload a rookie in the biggest game of his young career.

One more thing on Downs that I didn't understand at the time. In the fourth quarter against the Giants, his snap count dropped. If that was a reaction to the Likely touchdown, my message would be simple. Don't do that. If communication is already the problem, the last thing you do is take your best communicator off the field.

Change number three is the bluntest one. Do your job.

That's aimed at the veterans up front. Against the Giants, Dallas did not get nearly enough from Rashan Gary, Quinnen Williams, or Kenny Clark.

Quinnen Williams forced a fumble that Dallas recovered, and that was a genuinely impactful play. But outside of that, he managed a single pressure on the night. One. For a player of his caliber, against a Giants offensive line that isn't exactly elite, that number is jarring.

Kenny Clark didn't do enough to keep the linebackers clean behind him. And Rashan Gary, in his Cowboys debut after coming over from Green Bay, generated only a couple of pressures.

And here's why that matters even more in this specific scheme.

Dallas is planning to stop the run with lighter boxes. That means fewer defenders near the line of scrimmage and more in coverage. That only works if the defensive line wins its matchups up front consistently. If the big men get moved, there's no extra defender waiting to clean it up.

A light box with a losing defensive line is an open door.

And remember what this front cost. Gary came over in a trade that cost real future draft capital. Quinnen Williams just signed a market-setting extension. Kenny Clark came over in a trade last year to be exactly this kind of disruptive interior presence.

When you invest that much in a defensive front, the expectation isn't competent snaps. It's this group setting the tone for everybody else.

We don't have a full defensive breakdown from the Washington game in front of us. What I'd say is this. Holding Washington to twenty with that injury list tells you the effort and preparation are there. Whether the veterans up front actually turned the corner, we'll learn more when the full numbers come out, and a lot more in Rio.

So here's where I land.

The biggest change to Christian Parker's defense isn't a scheme adjustment. It's the back end of the secondary, and it was forced on him. Losing Malik Hooker and possibly P.J. Of the three changes Dallas can choose to make, the edge is the most urgent. Read option is the heart of Baltimore's offense, and the Giants already showed everybody where to attack. If the third edge defender hesitates in Rio, Lamar Jackson will run through that gap all afternoon.

The green dot needs stability, whoever ends up wearing it. And the veterans up front need to play like the money and draft picks this franchise spent on them.
