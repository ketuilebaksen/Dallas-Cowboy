Now I want to show you one more thing in this video, because it’s the most revealing football error in it and I don’t think anybody else will catch it.

The video offers two tactical scenarios. And the two scenarios describe two completely different players.

Scenario one makes him a dedicated third-down weapon. The language is a receiver back who sits in the flank, pedals into routes, lines up as the nickel back in the backfield, and the description is smaller, shiftier, a capable pass catcher with enough contact balance to win routes against a linebacker.

Scenario two makes him a tempo breaker on early downs, running outside zone and zone read, lining up outside the tackle, hitting the edge, reading the block, cutting into space created by a pulling guard.

Those are not two roles for one player. Those are two different players.

Let me explain why, because this is real football and it matters.

A third-down mismatch back is a route runner first. His job is winning two yards of separation against a linebacker in a phone booth. He lives on quickness in short spaces, hands, and body control. Size is almost irrelevant and sometimes a liability.

An outside zone tempo back is a vision and acceleration player. His job is reading three blocks while running full speed parallel to the line of scrimmage and deciding in a quarter of a second whether to bounce it, bang it, or cut it back. That requires patience, burst, and enough mass to run through contact at the edge.

Some backs can do both. Very few do both well, and the ones who do are among the most valuable offensive players in the league.

So when a video proposes both roles for the same man, with equal confidence, as interchangeable options, it’s telling you something. Nobody watched him play.

Let me explain this properly, because it’s the single most misunderstood thing about trades in the NFL.

A productive young player on a rookie contract is the most expensive asset class in professional football. Not the cheapest. The most expensive.

Here’s why. Value in a trade isn’t about how good somebody is. It’s about the gap between what he produces and what he costs. A thousand-yard back on a fourth-round rookie deal is producing starter output at backup money, for two more years, with no negotiation required.

That’s not a player a team sells. That’s a player a team builds around.

So when the video asks whether Dallas would pursue a targeted trade that subtracts a piece elsewhere to clear cap space, it’s solving the wrong problem. Cap space isn’t the obstacle. The obstacle is that Tampa Bay has no reason on earth to answer the phone.

And this is the thing fans consistently get backward. Availability is not a function of how much you want somebody. It’s a function of whether the other team has a reason to move him.

Remember my rule from the trade philosophy video. Shop for situations, not players.

A desperate team sells cheap. A hopeful team doesn’t sell at all. An unhappy great player costs less than a happy good one.

Bucky Irving is a happy, cheap, productive young back on a team that is not out of its season. There is no situation here. There’s only a wish.

But here’s the part nobody has reported properly, and I only got it from the head coach’s press conference.

Porter’s arrival has restructured the entire Dallas secondary.

Porter is now working the boundary, the short side of the field. And DaRon Bland has moved toward the field side, the wide side where there’s more grass to cover.

Those are different jobs. A boundary corner has the sideline helping him like a twelfth defender, so he can press and play aggressively. A field-side corner has far more space and needs more range and more discipline.

So one trade didn’t just add a player. It changed what Dallas’s existing best cornerback does for a living.

And it explains the thing that beat them in Houston. Dallas plays side-based assignments rather than having a corner follow one receiver everywhere. Houston put Nico Collins in the slot immediately, Dallas wasn’t expecting that much movement, and the structure got exposed.

That’s what a real addition looks like. It doesn’t just fill a hole. It reorganizes everything around it, and some of that reorganization costs you before it pays you.
