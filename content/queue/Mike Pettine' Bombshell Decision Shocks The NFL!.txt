Now the mechanism, and this is the part I want to teach you because it’s the detail that makes Pettine specifically interesting.

In particular, he likes simulated pressures. That means lining up linebackers in the double A gap, showing the quarterback a look that suggests a blitz, and making him guess who is coming and who is dropping into coverage.

Let me unpack that, because there are two separate ideas in it and both are important.

First, the double A gap.

The A gaps are the two spaces either side of the centre. One between the centre and the left guard, one between the centre and the right guard. They’re the shortest route to a quarterback on the field.

Put a linebacker in each one before the snap, and the centre has a problem that cannot be solved. He has two men in front of him and one body. He can block one. The guards have their own men. So somebody has to come free, and the offense has to decide, in about four seconds, which one.

Second, and this is the bit people miss. Simulated pressure is not extra pressure.

A simulated pressure sends the same number of rushers as a normal play. Four. But it sends different four. Two linebackers show in the A gaps, one rushes and one drops, and a defensive end who was lined up to rush drops instead.

So the quarterback is reading a protection scheme that doesn’t exist, and his five blockers are all accounted for against men who aren’t coming.

And here’s why that matters more than blitzing, and it’s the thing I taught you on the cover zero.

A blitz is a loan, not free aggression. You borrow from coverage to buy pressure. A simulated pressure borrows nothing. Four rush, seven cover, and the confusion is free.

Which is the direct answer to the sixty-six yard touchdown. Dallas sent everybody with no safety and McCaffrey caught a short pass and ran sixty-six yards into an empty field. A simulated pressure creates the same hesitation with the same number of rushers and a full secondary behind it.

Let me explain why that’s a real idea and not just moving a name around.

A nickelback plays over the slot receiver. It’s the hardest coverage job on the field for a safety-sized man, because slot receivers are quick and the space inside is enormous. You’re isolated, you have no sideline to help you, and you’re asked to cover a man built to change direction.

A strong safety plays closer to the line, reads the run first, and blitzes.

Now change number three, and this is the most technical thing in the video and the most valuable.

I would expect the Cowboys to lean more toward a one-gap approach.

So let me teach you this properly, because it’s the difference between two philosophies of defensive line play and it explains Quinnen Williams’s entire season.

In a one-gap defense, each defender is responsible for exactly one gap. One space. His job is to get into it, as fast and as violently as possible, and disrupt whatever is coming through it. He penetrates. He goes up the field. He doesn’t wait.

In a two-gap defense, a lineman takes on the blocker in front of him, controls him, and is responsible for both gaps either side. He reads and reacts. He holds ground rather than gaining it.

And the video tells me Dallas went to something in between.

The Cowboys planned to shift toward more of a one and a half gap approach, where players have a primary gap but also have to watch the secondary gap in case of a cutback.

One and a half gaps.

Now here’s why that’s the most consequential sentence in this video, and it’s a cost nobody mentions.

The half gap is paid for in hesitation.

Because a lineman with one gap goes. A lineman with a gap and a half has to confirm. And confirming takes maybe two tenths of a second, which doesn’t sound like anything until you remember that a defensive tackle’s entire advantage is arriving before the blocking scheme forms.

So a one-and-a-half gap scheme asks a penetrating lineman to penetrate slightly later, every single snap.
