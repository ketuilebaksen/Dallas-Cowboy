Now let me do the part that’s actually valuable, because the video is nominally about roster cuts and it clearly does not understand how they work. And this is genuinely useful, so stay with me.

The word cut does an enormous amount of misleading work in football coverage.

Here’s what actually happens at the end of August. A team has to get to fifty-three players on its active roster. Everybody else gets released. And a headline will tell you those men were cut, which sounds like the end of a career.

For most of them it’s a Tuesday.

Because here’s the mechanism. When a player with fewer than four accrued seasons is released, he doesn’t just become a free agent. He goes on waivers, which is a twenty-four hour window in which any other team can claim him, in a fixed order.

If nobody claims him, he clears waivers and he’s free to sign anywhere. And the overwhelmingly most common thing that happens next is that he signs to the practice squad of the team that just released him.

That’s the whole play. It’s sometimes called waive and hope, and it is a completely routine administrative step that moves a man from the fifty-three to the practice squad.

He’s still in the building. Still in meetings. Still practicing every day. Still getting paid, less. And still entirely available to be promoted when somebody gets hurt.

And I’ve documented exactly this on this channel. Michael Trigg, the tight end who was a Mackey Award finalist, was waived at the cutdown and Dallas brought him straight back to the practice squad because they wanted him in the building.

So cut, in late August, usually means demoted rather than departed.

And once you know that, look at what a headline is doing when it says star cut now. It’s taking the most routine procedural event in the football calendar and dressing it as an execution, because the procedure doesn’t get clicks and the execution does.

Now let me give you the rest of the mechanics, because they’re genuinely worth owning and today is the right day for it.

The practice squad is not a safe place. Any other team in the league can sign one of your practice squad players to their active roster at any time, and you cannot stop it. No compensation, no phone call.

Your only protection is four names a week. Each week a team designates four practice squad players as protected, and those four cannot be signed away. Everybody else is available to anybody.

And I covered a live example of that three days ago. Philadelphia signed Joe Milton, a quarterback, off the Dallas practice squad, because Dallas had not protected him. And I argued that was a defensible decision, because Dallas’s four slots were clearly pointed at a secondary in crisis.

And then the third mechanic, which is the cheapest tool in football. Elevations. You can elevate a practice squad player to play in a game without signing him to the fifty-three at all, three times per player per season. No roster spot sacrificed, no waiver exposure.

So the full picture is a ladder. Fifty-three, practice squad, protected or unprotected, elevated or not. And almost every transaction that gets reported as a dramatic cut is just a man moving one rung.

Now, the video does ask one genuinely good question, and I want to answer it properly, because nobody ever does.

It asks why a team would cut a rookie so soon. And it offers three possibilities. Was it scheme fit, did he struggle with the mental side, or was it simply a numbers game?

Those are the right three questions, and the answer involves money, so let me explain the economics of the bottom of a roster, because it’s genuinely counterintuitive.

A fifth-round pick’s contract is almost nothing.

Draft contracts are slotted by position, which means the money is determined by where you were picked rather than negotiated. First-rounders get fully guaranteed deals worth a great deal. By the fifth round, you’re looking at a modest signing bonus and base salaries near the league minimum, and almost none of it is guaranteed beyond that bonus.

Which produces a fact that surprises people. Below about the third round, your draft position buys you essentially no protection.

A team keeps a first-round pick on the roster through a bad camp because releasing him means eating a large guaranteed number and admitting an expensive mistake in public. A team keeps a fifth-round pick because it hopes he’s good. That’s the entire difference, and hope is a much weaker contract than money.

And then there’s the trap, which is the single most common mistake in roster building.

You do not keep a player because you drafted him.

That pick is already spent. It is gone, it cannot be recovered, and it does not care what you do next. Keeping a man on your fifty-three because of what you paid to get him is paying twice for the same decision, and the second payment comes out of a roster spot rather than a wallet.

Which brings me to the actual test, and this is the useful part.

At the bottom of a roster, the question is never is this player good. The question is, is this player better than the best man currently available on the street.

That’s it. That’s the whole evaluation. Because the fifty-three is a zero-sum ledger, which I’ve said all season. Every addition is a subtraction. So the man occupying spot fifty-one isn’t competing against a standard, he’s competing against whoever else you could sign on Tuesday afternoon.

And the honest counterweight, because this cuts both ways and the video half-gestures at it.

A team that releases every late-round pick at the first sign of struggle never develops anybody. Corners in particular take time, because the position is mostly recognition, and recognition is built from repetitions you can only get by playing badly first. If your policy is zero patience, you will be signing other teams’ developed players forever at other teams’ prices.

So the right answer is the boring one. You keep the ones whose specific problems look like inexperience and you release the ones whose problems look permanent. Technique and recognition are teachable. Speed and length are not.
