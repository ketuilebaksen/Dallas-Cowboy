Now, here’s where I’ll give the critics something real, because there is a genuine problem buried in this and it’s a better criticism than the one being made.

If you are paying thirteen million dollars a year, you are paying for a receiving weapon. That is what the market price for that position buys. Nobody pays top-of-market money for a man who catches footballs at the line of scrimmage and scores from the two.

This is not a production problem. It’s an allocation problem.

And that distinction matters enormously, because they have completely different solutions.

A production problem means the player can’t do the job, and you replace the player.

An allocation problem means the job doesn’t match the price, and you either change the job or you accept that you’re overpaying for a role player.

And here’s the trap I want you to avoid, because almost everybody is falling into it today. You can criticize the contract. You can criticize the usage. You cannot coherently criticize both and then also blame the player, because the player is the only one in that sentence doing what he was told.

Now let me deal with one more line in this video, because it crosses from analysis into something I won’t let pass.

It says some fans are arguing Dallas needs somebody who can handle the pressure of such critical moments.

No. I’m going to push back on that hard, and not because I’m being soft.

Drops are not a character trait.

The clutch framing is the least supported idea in all of sports commentary. Every serious attempt to find players who systematically perform better or worse in high-pressure moments, across enough attempts to mean anything, comes back with the same answer. The effect is tiny or nonexistent, and what people call clutch is almost entirely small samples remembered selectively.

Here’s the actual mechanism of a drop. A football arrives at a hundred twenty-something feet per second. A defender is closing. The catch window is roughly a tenth of a second. Everybody in the National Football League drops some of those, and the men who drop the fewest still drop several a year.

What makes a drop feel like a character failure is purely when it happened. The same hands, the same tenth of a second, the same inches. A drop in the first quarter is a statistic. The identical drop on the last drive is a referendum on a man’s soul.

That’s not analysis. That’s the scoreboard editing your memory.

So criticize the two drops. They were real, they were costly, and I led this video with them. But criticize them as drops. The moment you turn them into a claim about whether somebody can handle pressure, you’ve stopped describing football and started describing a feeling you had while watching it.

First. The contract.

Ferguson is signed through 2029 on fifty-two million dollars. And the video itself admits, in its own words, that with so much money tied up in him, making a move now might be difficult.

So the video raises a solution and then refutes it four sentences later. That’s not me being clever, that’s just reading it.

But let me explain the mechanics properly, because people genuinely misunderstand how this works.

When you cut or trade a player with a large signing bonus, you do not save the money. A signing bonus is paid up front and then spread across the contract years for cap purposes. The moment the player leaves, all of the remaining proration accelerates onto your cap immediately. That’s dead money.

Which means removing a well-paid player on a long deal in October typically costs you cap space rather than creating it. You pay him not to play, and you pay his replacement, and you’ve made your cap worse to make your roster worse.

So replace him isn’t a plan. It’s a mood.

Second. The 2027 draft.

Think about what that answer actually is. Dallas has a problem in October of 2026, and the proposed solution arrives in late April of 2027, then plays its first NFL snap in September of 2027, then becomes reliable somewhere in 2028.

That’s eighteen months to two years.

And I want to put this in the framework we’ve been using all month, because it exposes it completely.

I gave you the four levers a front office can pull, ranked by cost. Snap counts are free. The practice squad is nearly free. Street free agents are cheap. Trades are expensive.

Notice what isn’t on that list. The draft.

The draft is not a lever for this season. It never has been. It’s a lever for two seasons from now. Proposing the draft as the answer to a current problem is the analytical equivalent of answering a question about dinner by discussing agriculture.

So let me give you a rule out of all this, because I think it’s one of the most useful things I can hand you as a fan.

Any time somebody tells you a team should replace a player, ask three questions.

With whom. At what price. And what happens to the snaps in the meantime.

If they can’t answer all three, they haven’t proposed anything. And if the answer to any of them is the draft, they’ve answered a different question than the one you asked.

Replace him is the cheapest sentence in sports commentary, because it costs the person saying it absolutely nothing. It doesn’t have to name a successor, clear a cap, or cover a third down in November.
