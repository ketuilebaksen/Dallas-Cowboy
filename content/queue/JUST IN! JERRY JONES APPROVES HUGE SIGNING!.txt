Before the names, let me give you the framework, because the source I was handed today explains it properly and it’s the most useful thing in this whole video.

On a Wednesday injury report there are three designations.

A full participant is a green light. He did everything.

A limited participant did some work but not all of it.

And a DNP, which means did not participate, didn’t practice at all.

Now here’s the part that matters. A DNP can mean two completely opposite things. Sometimes it’s nothing, a veteran resting his legs. Sometimes it’s a flashing red warning light. And on a Wednesday you often cannot tell which.

So here’s the rule, and I’m adopting it permanently.

Wednesday is noise. Thursday is the truth.

And let me give you the mechanism, because the reason is specific and almost nobody explains it.

Wednesday is the first real practice after two days of treatment and recovery. Teams rest people on Wednesday as pure precaution all the time, because there’s no cost to it. Thursday is different. Thursday is typically the heaviest practice of the week, the main installation day, with the most team work and the most full-speed repetitions.

Which means if a player cannot get at least limited work on Thursday, he has not practiced in football pads at all that week. And almost nobody in professional football plays a Sunday game having taken zero team repetitions.

So Thursday is the deadline. That’s where the guessing ends.

That restraint is the right instinct, and I want to give you the full machinery behind it, because it’s the single most important idea in evaluating defensive players.

There are three completely different reasons a cornerback doesn’t get thrown at, and they mean opposite things.

Reason one. He’s good. Quarterbacks look at him and go elsewhere. That’s a compliment and it’s real.

Reason two. Somebody else is worse. The offense isn’t avoiding him, it’s hunting a different man, and he happens to be standing in a quiet part of the field. That says nothing about him at all.

Reason three. He isn’t on the field enough to be tested. A low snap count produces a clean stat line by arithmetic.

And here’s the problem. You cannot tell those three apart by looking at targets allowed, because all three produce the same number.

Regular viewers know I call this the hidden denominator. Every statistic is a rate multiplied by an opportunity, and if you only see the total you have no idea which half you’re looking at. I used it to explain Von Miller’s snaps, I used it to explain the pressure rate, and I used it four days ago to explain why Dallas ranks twentieth in pass defense while allowing the worst opponent passer rating in football.

So how would you actually tell? You look at targets per coverage snap rather than targets. And then you compare it to the other corner on the field. If both corners have low target rates and high snap counts, somebody’s good. If one is low and the other is high, the quarterback made a choice, and the low number is a compliment to the other guy’s weakness.

And now apply that to Dallas specifically, because the team context answers it.

Dallas allows over seventy percent completions in every single game this season, and has the worst opponent passer rating in the league.

Which means the footballs are going somewhere. Somebody is being picked on, relentlessly, every week.

Overshown did individual drills on Wednesday. But he did not do any team drills. And that’s why the official designation stayed a DNP.

Let me explain why that distinction is everything, because it’s the whole hamstring story.

Individual drills are position-specific, controlled work. You set the pace. You know what’s coming. You can run at ninety percent and nobody makes you change direction unexpectedly.

Team drills are full speed and reactive. You’re moving based on somebody else’s movement, which means sudden acceleration, sudden deceleration, and changes of direction you didn’t choose.

And here’s the thing about hamstrings. They don’t fail when you run. They fail when you decelerate hard or change direction suddenly, under load you weren’t braced for.

So individual drills tell you a man can run. Team drills tell you he can react. And a hamstring fails on reaction.

Which means passing individual drills is genuinely encouraging and genuinely not the test.
