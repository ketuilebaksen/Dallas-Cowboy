Now what a play caller actually does, because this is where I can be genuinely useful and almost nobody explains it.

The video’s opinion is the right one, so I’ll start there.

Shifting the play calling responsibility can be a meaningful move even when the coordinator keeps his title. The person making calls during a game has direct responsibility for choosing the defense’s response in those moments.

Correct. So let me tell you what those moments are.

A defensive play caller gets about twenty seconds between the end of one play and the next snap. In that time he has to see the offensive personnel coming onto the field, decide on a call, get it to the signaller, and have eleven men lined up before the ball moves.

And the job has three separable skills.

One. Anticipation. What is this offense likely to do on second and seven from their own thirty-five after a seven-yard gain.

Two. Response. The offense just scored on a reverse. Do you change what you’re doing, and how quickly.

Three. Risk management. When do you blitz, and crucially, when do you not.

And look at Sunday through those three.

Anticipation. San Francisco ran for two hundred and fourteen yards at over seven a carry, and Dallas allowed a seventy yard reverse. That’s anticipation.

Response. Twenty-nine points allowed in the second half after seventeen in the first, which means the adjustments at halftime went the wrong way.

Risk management. A cover zero blitz with Von Miller on Christian McCaffrey and nobody behind them, ten seconds after Dallas cut it to six.

So all three of the play caller’s skills failed in one game. Which is the strongest possible argument for the change Dallas just made, and it’s a better argument than the one I gave yesterday.
