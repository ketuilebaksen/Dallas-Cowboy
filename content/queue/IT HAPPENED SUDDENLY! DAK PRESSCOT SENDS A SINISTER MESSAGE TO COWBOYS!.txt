Stop. I want to explain why that number is so valuable, because almost nobody understands what it measures.

There are two similar-sounding statistics and they do completely different jobs.

Yards per attempt divides yards by every throw, including the incompletions. So it blends two separate things together: how accurate you are and how far you’re throwing. A quarterback can have a bad yards per attempt because he misses, or because he throws short, and the number can’t tell you which.

Yards per completion divides yards only by the throws that worked. Which means accuracy has been removed from the equation entirely.

So yards per completion measures exactly one thing. How far the ball is traveling when it is caught.

And that makes a career-low yards per completion a statement about the shape of this offense, not about the quarterback’s ability. The throws are shorter. That’s it. That’s what the number says and it’s all it says.

And I’ve made this argument before, on this channel, about one player. When everybody was piling on Jake Ferguson for six point eight yards per catch, I told you that six point eight yards per catch is a route tree, not a player. It’s a description of where he was asked to go, not how well he went there. Allocation, not production.

Today that same framework applies to the entire offense, with the quarterback’s own number instead of a tight end’s.

Now here comes the part I’m actually excited about, because the video presents two of these numbers as separate findings, one good and one bad, and they are not separate at all.

Three, you throw the ball away.

Number four is the one nobody mentions, and it is enormous. A pass rush needs about two and a half seconds to matter. Here’s the unfair half, and it’s a principle I want to give you, because it is the most abused move in all of sports argument.

They only won because of X is a sentence you can say about every football game ever played.

Every single game contains a dozen contingencies. A tipped ball. A spot. A third-string corner. A gust of wind. A hamstring. If you are willing to go looking for one, you will find one in every win, which means the method can discount any result you’d like to discount.

So here’s the rule. Call it asymmetric luck accounting.

If you are going to discount wins for luck, you have to discount losses for luck too. And almost nobody does, because the whole appeal of the method is that it only ever points one direction.

Watch it work on this video. It discounts both Dallas wins. It does not examine a single one of the four losses for contingency.
