Three reasons, and they get more decisive.

Reason one. The specific job Dallas needs is the one age takes first.

Think about what’s actually broken. Two hundred forty-one rushing yards allowed to Tampa Bay. A hundred sixty-eight point four per game, dead last in the league. And on Monday I explained that in a three-four front, the outside linebackers are the edge setters, and the failure is that nobody is holding the edge.

Now, setting an edge is not a finesse skill. It is the most physically brutal thing an edge rusher does. You take on an offensive tackle, often with a tight end attached to him, and you refuse to be moved while a running back decides whether to go inside or outside you. There is no technique that substitutes for the ability to hold ground against three hundred and twenty pounds.

Pass rushing ages reasonably well, because a veteran can beat you with hands and timing and a plan. Edge setting against the run does not age well at all, because it’s a strength contest and strength is the first thing to go.

Khalil Mack is thirty-five and has spent his career setting edges. Sixteen seasons is more mileage still, in the exact category where mileage is most expensive.

Reason two. Availability, and this is my single most repeated piece of trade philosophy. Shop for situations, not players.

The contract expires in January regardless. He has every reason to want out, they have every reason to let him go, and there is no future for either side to protect.

Reason three, and this is the one I actually care about, because it’s the trap in this whole proposal.

And I want to be fair. Leadership is real. It exists, it matters, and anybody who tells you it doesn’t has never been in a locker room.

But leadership is a terrible reason to make a trade, and here’s the precise reason why.

It’s the one quality you cannot verify from outside the building, cannot measure, and — most importantly — cannot collect quickly.

A veteran voice changes a culture over a season, or a career. It does not change a run fit in October. There is no amount of accountability that keeps a nose tackle from being displaced on a double team, and there is no motor in football that teaches gap discipline to ten other men in six days.

Dallas’s problem is not spiritual. It’s technical. Two hundred forty-one yards on thirty-nine carries, against a defense that knew the run was coming, because Baker Mayfield was out and Tampa Bay was protecting an undrafted rookie in his second start.

While I’m here, let me also do the thing the video refused to do, which is put actual numbers on the money, because it says cap reality, cap structure, dead money implications, contract length, guaranteed money and incentives, and it never once tells you an amount.

Here’s how in-season acquisitions actually work, and it’s genuinely simple.

When you trade for a player at the deadline, you only inherit the game checks that are left. Roughly half the season is gone, so a full-season figure of eight million becomes something closer to four. That’s why deadline rentals are cheap and why waiting is profitable.

What’s expensive is the future. If the contract runs past this season, you’ve bought years, not weeks, and that’s where dead money lives. A signing bonus is paid up front and spread across the contract for cap purposes, so if the player leaves early, all the remaining proration accelerates onto your cap at once.

Which is exactly why I said yes to Mack and no to Josh Sweat last week. Mack’s deal expires in January, so there is no future to buy. Sweat’s runs two more years at around seventeen million each, with a reported desire for a new contract on top. One of those is a rental. The other is a marriage proposal at rental prices.
