Now the complication, and this is mine.

Penalty counts are noisier than people think, and they’re partly a function of newness rather than character.

Look at what this defense actually is. A brand-new front structure that asks three linemen to two-gap. A rookie carrying the green dot. A cornerback who has been moved from the boundary to the field side inside three weeks. Two practice squad elevations in the secondary. A defensive coordinator in his first year and a senior assistant who arrived nine days ago.

New installs produce penalties. And the reason is mechanical rather than moral.

When a defender is thinking instead of reacting, he is late. And a late defender grabs, because grabbing is what you do when your feet didn’t get there. That’s holding, that’s illegal contact, that’s defensive pass interference. Those flags are usually a half-second of hesitation wearing a costume.

So eleven penalties might be a discipline problem, or it might be a symptom of a team that is still learning a system.

And here’s how you tell them apart, which is the useful part.

A discipline problem stays flat. If it’s about standards, the penalty count looks the same in December as it did in September, because nothing changed about who those people are.

An install problem declines. As the system settles and reaction replaces thought, the flags come down on their own.

So watch the penalty count across the next three weeks. Flat means Shariff is right and this is a culture issue. Falling means it was the install, and time was always the answer.
