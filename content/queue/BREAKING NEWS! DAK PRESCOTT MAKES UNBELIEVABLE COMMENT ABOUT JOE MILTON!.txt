First, a quick recap for anybody catching up, because the sequence here matters enormously.

Dallas went into cutdown day with a clear plan. Dak Prescott is the unquestioned starter. Behind him, a genuine backup competition between Sam Howell and Joe Milton that had been running since minicamp.

When the fifty-three was set, Dallas kept all three quarterbacks. Prescott, Howell, and Milton.

Then on Monday, the very next day, they waived Joe Milton.

And on the surface that looked harsh. This is a man who had just put together a genuinely impressive preseason. So a lot of fans reacted like the team had thrown away a talented young player for nothing.

But here's the detail that changes the entire story, and I want you to really sit with this one, because it's the smartest thing this front office did all week.

The timing was not an accident.

Think about what happens if Dallas cuts Milton on Sunday, on cutdown day itself. Every team in the league is finalizing rosters that same afternoon. They all have open spots. They're all shopping. A quarterback with Milton's arm and that preseason tape hits the wire alongside everybody else, and somebody very likely takes a shot.

Now think about what happens when you wait until Monday.

By Monday, every other team has already locked in their fifty-three. Claiming Joe Milton no longer means adding a player. It means turning around and cutting somebody you just told, twenty-four hours earlier, that he made the team. Somebody who already called his family.

That is a genuinely hard thing to ask a front office to do for a developmental quarterback.

And it worked exactly as designed. Milton cleared waivers. Not one of the thirty-one other teams put in a claim. Dallas re-signed him to the practice squad, kept him in the building, and didn't spend an active roster spot to do it.

Had they cut him a day earlier, he might be wearing somebody else's colors right now.

That's not luck. That's a front office that understands the calendar better than most.

Now let's get to the part of this story I actually want to spend time on, because it's the part that doesn't show up in a transaction wire.

Dak Prescott's response.

And let's be honest about why this matters practically, not just emotionally.

A practice squad quarterback who has checked out is worthless to you. He goes through the motions on the scout team, he doesn't study, and if you ever have to elevate him in November because your starter goes down and your backup got hurt in warmups, you find out very quickly that he spent ten weeks sulking.

A practice squad quarterback who stays locked in is an actual asset. He runs the scout team well, which makes your defense better every single week. He absorbs the offense. And he's genuinely ready if that phone call ever comes.

The difference between those two outcomes is largely determined by how the people around him handle the next few weeks.

And that's exactly why so many fans were confused. In their minds, the competition was simple. Whoever plays better wins. Milton played well. Milton should have won.

But that's not how backup quarterback evaluations work, and Brian Schottenheimer explained the reasoning pretty clearly.

Assessing the entire body of work, from training camp through the preseason, this staff felt that Sam Howell had executed the offense more consistently and shown more growth.

Execute the offense. That's the operative phrase, and it's doing a lot of work.

Because here's the thing about Joe Milton that has followed him since college. The arm is spectacular. It is genuinely one of the biggest arms in football. Scouts have been salivating over that arm since his college tape.

But the NFL is not an arm strength competition.

It's reading a defense before the snap and knowing what the safety rotation is telling you. It's calling an audible at the line when the look changes. It's processing progressions in under three seconds. It's throwing with anticipation to a spot where nobody is standing yet. It's ball placement, not ball velocity.

And those are the exact areas where Milton has struggled early in his NFL journey. Not because he isn't talented. Because those things take reps and time, and he simply hasn't had enough of either.

Meanwhile, Sam Howell has twenty NFL games and eighteen starts under his belt, all in Washington. He has been sacked in real games. He has read real defenses on real Sundays with real consequences.

That experience shows up as calm. And calm is what a coaching staff wants from the man who might have to walk onto a field cold in the second quarter of a road game.

Here's the way I'd put it. Milton is the higher ceiling with the lower floor. Howell is the lower ceiling with the much higher floor.

When your season depends on Dak Prescott's health the way this one does, you take the higher floor behind him. Almost every coaching staff in football makes that same call.
