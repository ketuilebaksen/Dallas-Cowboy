Here’s tell 36, and it’s the one I should have seen coming all week.

The headline promotes the job.

Not the man. The job. A suggestion for one role gets reported as an appointment to a bigger one, and because the name is real and the discussion is real, the lie hides inside a noun.

And the diagnostic is one question, and it takes two seconds.

When a headline names a job, check whether that’s the job the source named.

Because the gap between coordinator and head coach is not a detail. One of them runs eleven men on one side of the ball. The other runs the entire football operation, hires and fires the first one, and in this building currently calls the offensive plays himself. They are not adjacent positions. They’re different professions.

And look at the structure of the inflation, because it’s doing two things at once and only one of them is the obvious one.

Inflation one is the event. A pundit’s recommendation becomes a hire. That’s tell 32, which I built three days ago. A demand reported as an event.

Inflation two is the role. Coordinator becomes head coach. And that’s new, because it doesn’t touch the event at all. It upgrades the prize.

So you can falsify a headline twice over without inventing a single fake name, and today’s title does exactly that.

So here’s the principle, and I think it’s the most important thing this week has taught me.

A story that isn’t developing has to escalate.

That’s the mechanism. A real story grows because new facts arrive. A manufactured story has no new facts to grow on, and it still has to compete for the same attention tomorrow that it won today. The only variable left to turn up is the claim itself.

So the escalation isn’t evidence that something is happening. It is evidence that nothing is, because if something were happening, the facts would be doing the work.

And the practical version, which will save you a lot of grief. When a story’s headlines get louder across several days while the quotes inside them stay the same, you’re watching a story die noisily rather than develop.

Now here’s why those two resolutions matter today, rather than just being housekeeping.

Dallas already has a defensive coordinator. Dallas has also already added a senior defensive assistant who used to be an NFL head coach.

So Stephen A. Smith is proposing a third experienced defensive voice, for a staff that acquired its second one in the middle of this same season.

And I built a thing weeks ago about this, which I called the two-cooks problem, and today it becomes a three-cooks problem.

Here’s the mechanism. A defense needs one voice. Not because other opinions are worthless, but because a defense is a set of eleven interlocking assignments, and interlocking assignments require a single author. Two coaches who disagree about how to play a coverage do not produce the average of their two approaches. They produce a defense where four men play one version and seven play the other, and the quarterback throws into the seam between them.

So adding a third senior defensive voice to a staff that already has two is not obviously addition. It can be subtraction, and the video gets to this in its own words, which I’ll credit. Now hold the diagnosis next to the proposal.

Dallas’s failure was in-game adaptation. And in-game adaptation is the single thing a brand-new coordinator is worst at, for months.

Think about what adapting mid-game actually requires. It requires knowing which of your players can handle a different assignment on four words of instruction. It requires knowing who communicates well under crowd noise and who doesn’t. It requires knowing which of your coverages your personnel can execute without a week of practice, and which ones look fine on a board and fall apart on grass.

All of that is knowledge of specific human beings. None of it is scheme. And all of it takes a season to acquire.

So a new coordinator can bring you a better plan by Sunday. What he cannot bring you by Sunday is the ability to change the plan at halftime, because changing the plan at halftime is an act of knowing your players, and he’s known them for four days.

Which gives you the sentence that I think settles this.

A new coordinator is at his best at installation and at his worst at improvisation, and Dallas’s problem is improvisation.

So the proposal is aimed at the wrong failure. It would be a reasonable idea if Dallas were being beaten by superior schemes from the opening whistle. It is close to the opposite of what you’d want if your defense is good for three quarters and then cannot answer a substitution.

And I’ll give the honest counter, because there is one. Intensity is Smith’s word, and intensity is not scheme. If you believe the problem is that this defense stops competing when a game turns, then a new voice with a reputation for fire is a real intervention, and it works on a timescale of days rather than months.

I don’t believe that’s the problem, because ten points allowed in three quarters is not the signature of a unit that doesn’t compete. But I can’t disprove it from a box score, and anybody arguing it is arguing something coherent.

Although I’d run intensity through the inversion test before I accepted it as a diagnosis, because it doesn’t survive. Invert it. This defense needs less intensity. Nobody has ever said that about any football team in history, which means the word carries no information on its own. It only becomes a claim when you attach a mechanism to it, and the mechanism would have to be something like they stopped tackling in the fourth quarter, or they stopped communicating, which are checkable and which nobody has checked.

So if the problem really is intensity, the way to establish it is a tackling chart from the fourth quarter, not an adjective on a morning show.

And since I’ve spent a page saying what wouldn’t fix an adaptation failure, let me say what would, because criticism without an alternative is just complaining.

Three things, and none of them requires hiring anybody.

The first is pre-built answers for the substitution case. Every week, a defensive staff prepares for the opponent’s starting quarterback, because that’s who they expect to see. Almost nobody prepares a package for the backup, and the reason is that there’s no film on him. But you don’t need film to prepare a principle. You need a decision, made on Wednesday, about what you do differently if a pocket quarterback replaces a mobile one, or the reverse. That’s a one-page answer and it costs nothing, and the team that has it adapts in a timeout rather than in a quarter.

The second is the halftime process itself. Twelve minutes is not a long time to rebuild a plan, which is why good staffs don’t rebuild it. They arrive at halftime with two or three pre-approved adjustments and choose between them. The difference between a staff that adjusts well and one that doesn’t is usually not intelligence. It’s whether the adjustments were written down before kickoff.

The third is the one Dallas can actually do this week, which is to look at the film and establish whether those twenty points came from busted assignments or from beaten technique. Those have opposite fixes. A busted assignment is a communication problem and you fix it in a meeting room. Beaten technique is a practice-field problem and you fix it with repetitions. And nobody in four days of videos has told me which it was, which means nobody outside that building knows which problem Dallas has.

So the question I’d put to this coaching staff is not would you like a famous coordinator. It’s which of those two things happened in the fourth quarter, and what did you change on Wednesday because of it.
