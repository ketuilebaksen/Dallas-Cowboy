Now let me explain the three-four, because this is where everything connects and I think this is the most useful thing I’ll teach you this month.

A three-four front means three defensive linemen and four linebackers. A four-three means four linemen and three linebackers. Those sound like trivia. They’re not. They describe two completely different jobs for the men up front.

In a four-three, your linemen are generally one-gap players. You’re given one gap. Attack it. Penetrate, get upfield, win your side of the blocker, make something happen. It’s an aggressive, disruptive assignment, and the measure of success is getting past people.

In a three-four, your three linemen are generally two-gap players. And two-gapping is a fundamentally different and much harder thing.

Here’s the actual job. You take on a blocker, you control him, and you are responsible for the gap on both sides of him. You cannot just beat him. You have to beat him while staying square, so that whichever way the running back chooses, you’re still in position.

Why would you ask anybody to do that? Because of the arithmetic. Three linemen holding six gaps occupy five offensive linemen, which leaves four linebackers completely free to read the play and flow to the football. In theory, a well-played three-four is the hardest front in football to run against, because the men who tackle the ball carrier are never blocked.

In theory.

In practice, two-gapping is the single hardest technique in defensive football, and it is a technique, not an effort. You can be enormous, strong and willing and still be bad at it, because it requires you to win in a very specific shape.

Now, the honest risk, and this is the thing I haven’t heard anybody raise.

There is a specific failure mode when you add a senior voice above a young coordinator mid-season, and it has to do with the headset.
