A bad source does not make a good idea bad. And a good source does not make a bad idea good.

Adam Schefter putting Tony Pollard on a list of names to monitor is a fact about Tony Pollard’s availability. It is not an argument that Dallas should want him. Availability and fit are different questions, and most of the trade conversation in this sport collapses them into one.

Now the football, because Pollard is a good player and I’m not going to pretend otherwise.

He’s twenty-nine, on an expiring deal, a former Dallas fourth-round pick out of Memphis. He is not the lightning bolt he was at his peak. He is a reliable thousand-yard type who gives you around four yards a carry, and his game now is efficiency rather than fireworks.

That is a genuinely useful football player. And it is a description of a man Dallas already has.

Javonte Williams just scored three rushing touchdowns in the Houston game. He’s been healthy and productive all season. He is the engine room.

So the real question isn’t whether Pollard is better than Javonte Williams. It’s whether he’s enough of an upgrade over the backup to pay what he costs.

And the money here is the whole argument, so let me walk you through it, because the mechanics are worth understanding.

His cap figure this season is around nine point two six million. The number a new team would inherit is roughly seven point two five million, but that’s the full-season figure. A trade in late October or early November only picks up the remaining game checks, so the real number lands closer to four million.

Four million dollars for a backup running back behind a healthy, productive starter.

And here’s the comparison that ends it, which the video makes and I want to amplify. Dallas is paying Joey Porter Junior less this season than Pollard would cost in prorated money.

A starting boundary cornerback who changed the structure of the defense costs less than the backup running back.

So let me give you the principle, because this is the actual lesson and it applies far beyond football.

You don’t buy insurance at starter prices for a risk that isn’t live yet.

If Javonte Williams goes down, Pollard becomes a sensible target immediately, and the market will still exist, because running backs are the one position where supply never dries up. The deadline is November tenth. There is a full month between now and then, and prices fall as it approaches.

Right now the risk is theoretical and the price is real. That’s a bad trade every single time.

Bottom line on Pollard. Right player, wrong moment, and the romance of bringing him home is not a football argument. It’s a feeling, and I like the feeling too.

Now the one I actually want to talk about. Khalil Mack.

Yes. Enthusiastically yes. This is the best-fitting trade idea anybody has put in front of me all season.

Here’s the situation, and watch how cleanly every piece lines up.

Mack is thirty-five, which is ancient for an edge rusher, and he’s still producing. Multiple sacks and several tackles for loss already this season, which, as the video correctly notes, is more than a lot of edge rushers can say.

He re-signed with the Chargers on a one-year deal this offseason for one stated reason. He wanted to chase a championship.

And the Chargers are zero and four.

Stop right there, because that is the entire trade.

Regular viewers know my single most repeated piece of trade philosophy. Shop for situations, not players.

A desperate team sells cheap. A hopeful team doesn’t sell at all. And an unhappy player on a team going nowhere is the cheapest asset in football.

Khalil Mack is the purest version of that I have ever seen. A thirty-five-year-old on an expiring one-year deal, who signed specifically to win a ring, on a team that is zero and four. He has every reason to want to leave. They have every reason to let him. And there is no future to protect on either side, because the contract ends in January regardless.

Schefter’s framing was that the Chargers need to turn it around to keep him, which is the polite professional way of saying he’s gone if the losing continues.

Now the price, which is the part that makes me genuinely excited.

A fourth-round pick at most. Possibly less.

And on the money, the Chargers save around eight million because they’re eating the signing bonus proration, which means the acquiring team picks up a prorated figure closer to four million by deadline time.

Four million dollars and a day-three pick for a productive veteran edge rusher.

Now let me tell you why this is better than the argument I made two days ago, because I want to be honest about my own analysis improving.

Two days ago I endorsed trading a fifth-round pick for Jadeveon Clowney, and my reasoning was specific. I said Dallas does not need a better pass rusher. It needs a pass rusher it can trust on first down. I used Von Miller’s run defense grade of sixty-six point four to make the point that Dallas has a specialist it cannot play on early downs, which is exactly why Miller has only played sixty-two snaps in four games.

Khalil Mack is that same argument, upgraded.

Because Mack has never been a third-down-only player. He’s an every-down edge who has spent his entire career setting an edge in the run game as well as rushing the passer. That is precisely the hole I identified. Dallas doesn’t need another situational rusher to stand next to Von Miller. It needs somebody who can be on the field on first and ten.

And it connects directly to what the head coach said last week. He told us the pass rush is downstream of coverage and downstream of early-down defense, and that you can’t let quarterbacks post passer ratings around a hundred thirty and expect the front to fix everything. The way you create the third-and-longs that a Von Miller exists for is by stopping people on first and second down.

Mack helps with that. A one-dimensional speed rusher does not.

Now the honest counter-argument, because I always give you one.

He’s thirty-five. The production could taper as the season grinds on. A veteran in his final stretch can only give you so much, and there is no hiding that.

And here’s my answer. Age is a problem when you’re paying for the future. You are not. You are renting roughly ten games and a playoff run from a player whose contract expires in January, for a day-three pick.

For a rental, the only question that matters is whether he’s good right now. The video says multiple sacks and several tackles for loss early this season. That’s a yes.

Also, and this matters, Dallas is light on draft picks. They don’t have many this year and they already spent 2028 capital on the Porter trade. Which means premium picks are off the table and day-three picks are exactly the currency they can still afford to spend.

So the price fits the wallet, the player fits the hole, and the seller has every reason to sell. That’s three out of three, and I’ve never said yes faster.

Now the hard one. Josh Sweat.

And I’m going to say no. But not for the reason you’d guess, and the reason is the most interesting thing in this entire video.

Let’s start with the fact that he’s better. Because he is, and nobody should pretend otherwise.

Twelve sacks in 2025. His second double-digit sack season. At least six sacks every single year since 2020. And he’s a plus run defender, which is the exact quality I just said Dallas needs. He just had his best game of the season, finally getting the sack he’d been chasing plus two tackles for loss.

As a football player, he’s the better addition. No debate.

And the fit is better than the fit, if that makes sense, because of two details that are the best pieces of reporting in the whole video.

First, Sweat has ties to Christian Parker, the Dallas defensive coordinator, because the two briefly overlapped in Philadelphia.

Second, he has played in Vic Fangio’s scheme. Which, if you’ve watched this channel, you know is the exact family of principles Dallas is trying to run. Keep it in front of you, rush four and cover seven, take away the explosive play.

Put those together and you get the thing I said about Porter two weeks ago. Defense is a language, and a man translating in his head plays slower than a man who knows the words. Josh Sweat would arrive already speaking it.

So why no?

Three reasons, and the third one is the one I want you to remember.

Reason one. The price is two third-round picks. For a team the video correctly describes as short on draft capital and already into its 2028 supply. That’s a premium price from a thin wallet.

Reason two. The contract. It runs through the next two seasons after this one, with around seventeen million due in each of those years. So this isn’t a rental. This is a two-year commitment, acquired at rental time, at full price.

And reason three. Schefter reported that Josh Sweat wants a new contract.

Now. A man who signed his deal one offseason ago is apparently looking to tear it up.

And here’s where I have to add something to our framework, because I gave you a rule weeks ago that this situation breaks.

I told you an unhappy great player sells for less than a happy good one. That’s true, and I stand by it as a general principle.

But it has an exception, and this is it.

An unhappy player sells cheap when his unhappiness is about his situation. The losing. His role. The city. The coaching. Those are things a new team fixes for free, just by being a different place. You inherit a motivated player and you pay nothing for the motivation.

An unhappy player does not sell cheap when his unhappiness is about his money. Because then you aren’t buying a player. You’re buying a negotiation.

You pay the picks to Arizona, and then you pay the raise to him. The discount doesn’t go to you. It goes to the player, and you fund it.

So here’s the new rule, and write it down, because it’s going to be useful every single deadline for the rest of your life as a fan.

Unhappy about the situation is a discount. Unhappy about the contract is a surcharge.

Now let me do the quote, because it’s genuinely a masterclass and almost nobody will read it correctly.

Sweat was asked about the report after the Cardinals game on Sunday. He said he didn’t know where Schefter got that from. He said he hadn’t said anything. He said he’s focused on winning games, not on contracts.

Now read what’s missing.

He never said it wasn’t true.

He denied being the source. He did not deny the substance.

And that distinction is one of the most reliable tells in sports reporting, so let me give you the rule.

When a player denies the sourcing instead of the claim, the claim usually survives.

Here’s the mechanism. If a report is false, denying it costs you nothing. You say that’s not true, I’m happy here, and it’s over. Easy, free, and it ends the story.

Denying only the leak is a much more careful answer. It protects a relationship with a reporter, it protects the player from being called a liar later, and it leaves the underlying fact completely intact.

So which is it? Maybe Sweat wants out. Maybe he wants more money. Maybe the Cardinals are leaking it themselves to manufacture trade pressure, which teams absolutely do. Or maybe it’s noise.

But I’d bet the want-a-new-deal part is closer to true than false, purely on the shape of that answer.

And now the detail I think is the smartest observation in the video, which I want to credit properly and then build on.

Arizona’s general manager may be on a warm seat. They got blown out by Seattle thirty-one to seven a few weeks ago. And the point made was this. General managers who are fighting for their jobs often don’t trade their best players, because they’re trying to save the season.

That is a genuinely sophisticated piece of front-office psychology and it’s correct.

Let me explain why, because it’s the kind of thing that makes you understand deadlines properly.

Trading your best defensive player is a public announcement that the season is over. And the man who makes that announcement is also announcing that his own roster failed. A general manager with a secure job can make that call, because he’ll be there to spend the picks. A general manager who might not be employed in February cannot, because the picks arrive for somebody else and the surrender is on his record.

So the incentive is backwards. The more desperate the team’s record, the more desperate the GM, and the less likely he is to do the rational thing.

Which means even if Dallas wants Josh Sweat, Arizona may simply say no, and the entire conversation is academic.

That’s why Mack feels realistic and Sweat feels exciting. Those are different words and the difference is the whole game.

Now, the thing on this list that nobody named, and I think it’s the best unprompted point in the video.

Linebacker.

Schefter didn’t name a single linebacker. But Dallas is starting a rookie at linebacker, and the video raises the dreaded rookie wall as a winter risk.

I want to expand that, because it’s real and it’s physiological rather than mental.

A college football season is twelve or thirteen games. An NFL season, with a preseason in front of it, is well over twenty weeks of full-contact work against bigger people. A rookie’s body has never done that. Not once. There is no training for it except doing it.

So the wall is not a loss of motivation. It’s a cumulative load that arrives around November, and it shows up as half a step of speed and a quarter second of processing.

And here’s why it matters specifically for Jaishawn Barham, and this is my own point rather than the video’s.

Barham isn’t just playing. He’s carrying the green dot. He’s relaying defensive calls. And he’s learning brand new coverage responsibilities, counting receiving threats from the outside inward on every single snap, in about two seconds, before the ball moves.

Fatigue degrades processing before it degrades speed. Always. A tired player can still run. A tired player cannot think as fast.

Which means the first thing the rookie wall takes from Barham is the exact skill he’s newest at.

And now look at what Schottenheimer told us last week, and tell me it doesn’t look like foresight. He said Dallas has trained multiple defenders to handle the green dot and relay the calls, and he admitted they didn’t have enough alternatives prepared last year when Jack Sanborn wasn’t available.

Everybody read that as injury insurance. I think it’s also wall insurance. You build redundancy in the communication chain so that in late November you can take some mental load off a rookie without taking him off the field.

That’s a staff planning three months ahead. I’ll take it.

So, the big question. Does Thursday night change any of this?

And my answer is going to annoy some people. Mostly no.

Here’s why. Dallas lost that game to three giveaways. Two interceptions and a fumbled kick return that became a touchdown. That is a drive-termination problem, and I spent an entire video yesterday explaining that it’s a different failure mode than inefficiency.

Now go back through this list and ask which of these three players fixes a tipped interception.

None of them. Not one.

So the loss should not change the shopping list. The shopping list was already defensive, and the correct conclusion before Thursday was the same as the correct conclusion after it. The video’s own closing logic is right. The offense has proven it can score on anybody. The defense is the weak link. That’s where the resources go.

What the loss should change is the urgency narrative, and I want you to be careful with that, because urgency is how front offices overpay.

A team that just got embarrassed is the team most likely to make a bad trade, because somebody in the building wants to do something. And a month before the deadline, doing something is the most expensive possible instinct.

Prices fall as November tenth approaches. They always do, because sellers run out of reasons to hold on. Patience is the cheapest asset a front office owns, and Dallas is about to have ten days with nothing to do but think.

So here’s my board, with prices, so you can hold me to it.

Marvin Harrison Junior. No. Dallas has two receivers and one football.

Jerry Jeudy. No. Same answer.

Tony Pollard. No, at four million and any pick, while Javonte Williams is healthy. Yes immediately if that changes.

Khalil Mack. Yes. At a fourth. Yes at a third if somebody else is bidding. Best fit on the board and the clearest seller in football at zero and four.

Josh Sweat. No at two thirds plus a new contract. The player is better and the situation is worse, and Arizona probably won’t sell him anyway.

And that’s my record now stretching back a month, consistent the whole way. No to two first-round picks for a receiver. Yes to a 2028 second for Porter, which happened. Yes to a fourth for a backfield piece. Yes to a fifth for Clowney. No to a premium pick for a Tampa Bay running back. Yes to a fourth for Khalil Mack.

One philosophy, one sentence. The question is never whether the player is good. The question is always whether the price matches the problem.
