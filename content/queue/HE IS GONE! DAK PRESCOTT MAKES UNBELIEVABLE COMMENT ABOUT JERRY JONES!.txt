Now let me give you the actual football problem Dallas has to solve on Sunday night, because I think I finally understand why the numbers look the way they do, and it’s a choice rather than a failure.

Here’s the puzzle I laid out a few days ago. Dallas is dead last against the run and twentieth in pass defense. And I explained that the pass defense rank is being flattered, because a team that runs for a hundred sixty-eight yards a game doesn’t need to throw, so the passing yards stay artificially low.

That’s true. But today I want to give you the cause rather than the symptom, and it comes down to one decision that gets made before every single snap.

How many defenders do you put in the box?

The box is the area right behind the line of scrimmage, roughly between the offensive tackles. Those are the defenders who can realistically stop a running play. Everybody else is deeper, covering receivers.

And it’s a fixed-sum problem. You have eleven men. Every one you pull out of the box to help against the pass is one less body at the point of attack against the run. Every one you add to the box is one less defender in coverage.

Now, Dallas has been running a defense built on Vic Fangio principles, which I’ve explained on this channel repeatedly. Keep it in front of you. Rush four, cover seven. Take away the explosive play above all else. And we have the evidence that they’re committed to it, because the head coach told us this defense has played its highest rate of cover three all season, as the simplification everybody asked for.

That philosophy requires safeties playing deep. Which means a lighter box.

So look at what you get. A light box plus a two-gap three-four front, where three linemen are being asked to control six gaps so that four free linebackers can flow to the football.

That structure works beautifully when the linemen win their gaps. And when they don’t, there is nobody behind them, because the extra body you’d normally have in the box is forty yards downfield taking away a throw nobody is attempting.

That’s how you end up thirty-second against the run and twentieth against the pass at the same time. It isn’t incompetence. It’s a trade that stopped paying.

Dallas is paying a premium every single snap to prevent explosive passing plays, against opponents who have figured out they don’t need to throw at all.

And here’s the thing about Sunday night. Green Bay can run it. So the trade gets tested in the worst possible place.

Load the box. Bring a safety down. Accept that you will occasionally get beaten over the top, because getting beaten over the top once for forty yards is cheaper than getting beaten on the ground thirty-nine times for two hundred forty-one.

That’s the arithmetic. One explosive play is a worse single event and a much better outcome than a four-hour grinding surrender of possession.
