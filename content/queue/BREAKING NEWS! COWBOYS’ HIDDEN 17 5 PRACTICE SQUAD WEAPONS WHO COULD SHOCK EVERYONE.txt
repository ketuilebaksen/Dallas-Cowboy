Player one. Wanya Morris, offensive tackle.

Let me start with what Brian Schottenheimer actually said at his press conference. His words.

A guy we really liked the year Guyton was coming out. He was on the opposite side of Tyler and a guy that we've seen play some good ball. We love the athleticism. We like the size.

This coaching staff has been watching Wanya Morris for three years, since before he was even drafted. And now he's on their practice squad.

Here's the situation fast. Forty-three NFL games. Sixteen starts. He protected Patrick Mahomes. He won a Super Bowl. He's twenty-five years old.

Atlanta cut him before the season, he cleared waivers, and Dallas swooped in the next morning. One bad training camp is the entire reason he was available.

And look at the tackle room he's walking into. Guyton is locked in on the left. On the right, Braxton Jones and Terence Steele are competing and neither one is settled. Rookie Drew Shelton is on the fifty-three but multiple analysts have noted he isn't ready for a significant role.

We flagged tackle depth as a genuine problem on this channel weeks ago. It hasn't been solved.

Nick Harris of the Fort Worth Star-Telegram put it plainly. Pretty solid addition to have on a practice squad. Would imagine he finds his way onto the fifty-three at some point this year as the backup left tackle.

Inside The Star was even more direct. They called Morris the first name on the entire seventeen-man squad they'd watch to make the fifty-three first.

And here's the detail that sticks with me. Morris last played a regular season game at AT&T Stadium on Thanksgiving, when Dallas beat Kansas City thirty-one to twenty-eight. He knows that building. He has been thrown into real NFL games knowing defenses were coming after him.

Six-six, three hundred seven pounds, and he can move. Schottenheimer used the words "more athletic" describing what these moves did to the tackle room.

That is not a depth signing. That's a coach going and getting a player he actually wants. There's a difference, and I don't think Morris spends this whole season on the practice squad.

Player two. Reddy Steward, defensive back.

Something happened the week before final cuts that I need you to know, because once I explain it, it's going to drive you a little crazy.

Schottenheimer stood in front of reporters and said this.

Can't speak enough about a guy like Reddy Steward. Guy moves from nickel. He plays at safety. He does everything right. He's intelligent. He's a great communicator. Those guys are hard to find sometimes.

Those guys are hard to find.

He said that, and then released him four days later.

I'm not bashing the decision. Roster construction is brutal and sometimes you cut players you love because the math doesn't work. But hold onto that quote, because it tells you exactly where this goes.

Last season Steward played all seventeen games in this system and started five. Five hundred nineteen defensive snaps. The only Cowboys cornerback who played more was DaRon Bland. He finished with forty tackles, a sack and a half, three passes defensed, and a forced fumble.

This offseason Dallas brought him back on an exclusive rights tender. They controlled that entirely. And then they spent camp adding safety to an already versatile profile. Nickel, outside corner, safety, special teams.

The room just got too crowded. Kobe Durant is the nickel now. Devin Moore was drafted. The numbers didn't work.

But DaRon Bland, who just signed a four-year, ninety-two million dollar extension, has now had surgery on the same left foot in back-to-back years. The same bone, two years running. He came back healthy for camp, yes. But that foot has history, and this is a long violent season.

And this isn't hypothetical. It already happened in 2025. Bland got hurt, the door opened, and Schottenheimer said publicly you're going to see Reddy Steward play a lot of football.

Same coach. Same player. Same fragility. The pattern is already written.

Schottenheimer doesn't say "hard to find" about a player he's walking away from. Every move this offseason pointed toward keeping Steward. The numbers just didn't cooperate.

He cleared waivers too. Every team had a shot. Nobody took him. Dallas got him back.

Reddy Steward is one Bland injury away from starting NFL games. That's not a bold prediction. That's reading the room.

Player three. Justin Barron, linebacker.

August eleventh. Joint practice with the Rams in Woodland Hills.

Justin Barron intercepts three passes. Three. Including one off Matthew Stafford with the starters on the field. He also makes multiple run stops in the same session.

The Dallas Sports Journal called it National Justin Barron Day. Blogging The Boys said he went from roster bubble to near-lock overnight.

And then he got cut anyway.

Barron is a twenty-four-year-old second-year linebacker out of Syracuse who played safety for four years in college before switching to linebacker as a senior, where he led the team with ninety-three tackles.

That safety background is everything. It's why he has coverage instincts most linebackers in this league simply don't have. It's why he picked off Stafford.

And it's why this staff trusted him with the green dot in preseason. The play-calling communicator.

You do not hand that helmet to a bubble player. That's the guy who processes the call, communicates pre-snap adjustments, and keeps eleven players aligned in real time against motion-heavy offenses. Barron wearing it tells you they see somebody who understands this system at a level most backups never reach.

So why was he cut? Because that linebacker room is genuinely loaded. Overshown, Dee Winters, Jaishawn Barham, Shemar James. Four names, limited spots.

This was not a talent decision. It was arithmetic.

And those four carry real risk. Overshown has dealt with significant knee issues. Barham is a rookie still developing. Winters just arrived in a trade.

One injury to that top four and Barron gets the call. Keep this name close.

Player four. Michael Trigg, tight end.

Here's a number. Two hundred eighty thousand dollars. That's roughly the guaranteed money Dallas put into Trigg's undrafted free agent deal.

For context, another Cowboys undrafted receiver that some analysts thought might get drafted got around a hundred five thousand guaranteed.

Trigg got nearly triple that. In the undrafted world, that's not a signing. That's a statement.

He's long, athletic, exactly the receiving tight end profile every offense in this league is hunting. The reason he slipped undrafted was character concerns and a deep tight end class. He served a suspension at Ole Miss before transferring. Teams had questions. Most passed. Dallas didn't.

And then something happened in camp that nobody expected from a player with that file.

He worked on his blocking. The exact thing scouts were skeptical about. A beat reporter noted on August sixth, unsolicited, mid-camp, that Trigg looked better as a blocker. That kind of observation from somebody at every practice means people noticed improvement.

In the preseason opener he played thirty-two offensive snaps, caught four of five targets for thirty yards. Steady. Didn't disappear. Didn't drop anything.

Tight ends coach Lunda Wells went on record saying he's excited about Trigg's future. Coaches don't single out undrafted tight ends unless they mean it.

And Schottenheimer did it too, back at rookie minicamp. His words. You talk about a guy that can stretch the field vertically and make game-changing, wow-type plays attacking the middle of the field.

That's a head coach going out of his way in May to tell you about a player who hadn't taken a snap.

The roster math is real too. Jake Ferguson is TE1. Brevyn Spann-Ford is TE2. And Luke Schoonmaker is TE3 on a contract that creates cap savings if he's released. We've been documenting Schoonmaker slipping since the first depth chart.

Trigg is sitting right there. Watch this name in October.

Player five. Israel Abanikanda, running back.

This one comes down to a single question. Why did the Cowboys keep him?

On cutdown day, Dallas released Jaydon Blue, their own draft pick. Released Phil Mafah, another draft pick. Let both of their own guys walk.

And in the middle of all that, they kept Israel Abanikanda, a player who has bounced through the Jets, the Niners, and the Packers in three years.

They chose him over their own draft picks. That is a significant tell.

He's twenty-three, fast, and explosive, and he spent this entire offseason in the building learning the system. That's the whole point. He's not somebody you have to bring up to speed in an emergency.

And look at the backfield. Javonte Williams is the starter, and while he had a career-saving 2025 season, remember he tore his ACL, LCL, and posterolateral corner back in 2022. Three ligaments. He's carrying a workhorse role again in a violent league.

Malik Davis is the backup. Dallas claimed another back off waivers. That room is one play from a real problem.

But here's why I think they specifically kept Abanikanda over Blue, and nobody's saying this out loud.

Look at what this staff is building at running back. Downhill runners. Power between the tackles. Dependability. The room has a style.

Abanikanda is different. He's the one who hits a crease and is gone. He put up three hundred twenty yards in a single college game because he has a gear nobody else on that field could match.

I think Schottenheimer kept him because he wants that weapon available. Not every game. But the moment this offense needs one play to flip a fourth quarter, the answer is already in the building.

That's a coaching staff thinking three moves ahead.
